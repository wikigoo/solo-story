***Hamlet*** ([/ ˈ h æ m l ɪ t /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")) is a [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy "Tragedy") written by [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare "William Shakespeare") sometime between 1599 and 1601. It is Shakespeare's longest play. Set in [Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark "Denmark"), the [play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theatre\) "Play (theatre)") depicts [Prince Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet "Prince Hamlet") and his attempts to exact revenge against his uncle, [Claudius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Claudius "King Claudius"), who has murdered [Hamlet's father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_\(Hamlet\) "Ghost (Hamlet)") in order to seize his throne and marry [Hamlet's mother](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_\(Hamlet\) "Gertrude (Hamlet)").

*Hamlet* is considered among the "most powerful and influential tragedies in the English language", with a story capable of "seemingly endless retelling and adaptation by others".[^14] It is widely considered one of the greatest plays of all time.[^15] Three different early versions of the play are extant: the [First Quarto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_Q1 "Hamlet Q1") (Q1, 1603); the Second [Quarto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarto "Quarto") (Q2, 1604); and the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio "First Folio") (F1, 1623). Each version includes lines and passages missing from the others.[^16] Many works have been pointed to as possible sources for Shakespeare's play, from ancient [Greek tragedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_tragedies "Greek tragedies") to [Elizabethan dramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_drama "Elizabethan drama").

## Plot

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:KronborgCastleDenmarkOct152022_06.jpg)

Kronborg Castle is immortalised as Elsinore in the play Hamlet

### Act I

[Prince Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet "Prince Hamlet") of Denmark is the son of the recently deceased [King Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_\(Hamlet\) "Ghost (Hamlet)") and nephew of [King Claudius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Claudius "King Claudius"), his father's brother and successor. Claudius married King Hamlet's widow and Prince Hamlet's mother, [Gertrude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_\(Hamlet\) "Gertrude (Hamlet)"). Denmark has a long-standing feud with neighbouring [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway"); Denmark fears an invasion led by the Norwegian king's son, [Fortinbras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortinbras "Fortinbras").

Prince Hamlet's friend [Horatio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_\(Hamlet\) "Horatio (Hamlet)"), and two sentries, witness the ghost of King Hamlet and resolve to tell Prince Hamlet. The court gathers the next day and Claudius berates Hamlet for continuing to grieve for his father. Hamlet bemoans his father's death and his mother's hasty remarriage. Learning of the ghost from Horatio, Hamlet resolves to see it himself.

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:File-Hamlet,_Prince_of_Denmark_Act_I_Scene_IV.png)

Horatio, Hamlet, and the ghost (Artist: Henry Fuseli, 1789) 17

[Ophelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophelia_\(character\) "Ophelia (character)") has been wooed by Hamlet. Her brother [Laertes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laertes_\(Hamlet\) "Laertes (Hamlet)"), who is leaving for France, warns her against returning Hamlet's affection, and then her father [Polonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonius "Polonius") orders her to reject Hamlet's advances. That night, the ghost appears to Hamlet and tells him that he was murdered by Claudius: demanding that Hamlet avenge him, which Hamlet vows to do. The prince confides to Horatio that he plans to act as though he has gone mad and swears Horatio and the sentries to secrecy. Hamlet remains uncertain of the ghost's reliability.

### Act II

Ophelia tells Polonius that Hamlet had appeared at her chamber and behaved erratically; Polonius concludes that Hamlet's madness is due to love. Meanwhile Fortinbras has been ordered by Norway to abandon an invasion of Denmark but requests passage through Denmark for a march on [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland").

Polonius speaks to Hamlet and the latter feigns madness. [Rosencrantz and Guildenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosencrantz_and_Guildenstern "Rosencrantz and Guildenstern"), two of Hamlet's friends, arrive; Hamlet discerns that they are there to spy on him. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern tell Hamlet that they have brought a troupe of actors. Hamlet asks the actors to stage *The Murder of Gonzago*, a play featuring a death in the style of his father's murder. Hamlet intends to study Claudius's reaction to the play and determine the truth of the ghost's story.

### Act III

Polonius orders Ophelia to return Hamlet's love tokens, while he and Claudius secretly watch to evaluate Hamlet's reaction. Hamlet accuses Ophelia of immodesty, denying he ever loved her, convincing Claudius that Hamlet is not mad for love. The court assembles to watch the play Hamlet has commissioned, and Claudius abruptly rises and runs from the room which, for Hamlet, proves his uncle's guilt.

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hamlet_stabs_Polonius.jpg)

Hamlet mistakenly stabs Polonius (Artist: Coke Smyth, 19th century).

Claudius tries to pray, but cannot repent. Hamlet sneaks behind Claudius but does not kill him, reasoning that killing him at prayer will send him to heaven. In the queen's bedchamber, Hamlet and Gertrude fight. Polonius, who is spying on the conversation behind an arras, calls for help and Hamlet stabs wildly, killing Polonius. Hamlet brutally insults his mother, but the ghost enters and reprimands Hamlet for his harsh words. Unable to see or hear the ghost, Gertrude takes Hamlet's conversation as further evidence of madness.

### Act IV and V

The king sends Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to accompany Hamlet to England with a sealed letter to the English king requesting that Hamlet be executed. Ophelia is driven mad by her father's death. Laertes arrives back from France, enraged, and witnesses Ophelia's madness. Claudius convinces Laertes that Hamlet is responsible. A letter arrives indicating that Hamlet has returned to Denmark. Claudius proposes a fencing match between Laertes and Hamlet. Laertes will be given a poison-tipped foil, and, if that fails, Claudius will offer Hamlet poisoned wine. Gertrude arrives with news that Ophelia has drowned.

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hamlet_et_Horatio_au_cimeti%C3%A8re_-_Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_1942_-_photo_2.jpg)

The gravedigger scene. 18 (Artist: Eugène Delacroix, 1839)

Hamlet and Horatio happen upon Ophelia's funeral, and Laertes and Hamlet fight. Back at the castle, Hamlet agrees to the fencing challenge from Laertes. Gertrude raises a toast to him using the poisoned wine. Claudius tries to stop her but she drinks. Laertes then slashes Hamlet with his poisoned blade. In the ensuing scuffle, they switch weapons, and Hamlet wounds Laertes. Gertrude dies and Laertes reveals Claudius's plan. Hamlet kills Claudius. Hamlet dies in Horatio's arms, naming Fortinbras as his successor.

## Sources

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saxo_original_001.jpg)

A facsimile of Gesta Danorum by Saxo Grammaticus, which contains the legend of Amleth

*Hamlet* -like legends are so widely found (for example in Italy, Spain, Scandinavia, Byzantium, and Arabia) that the core "hero-as-fool" theme is possibly [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans "Proto-Indo-Europeans") in origin.[^19] Several ancient written precursors to *Hamlet* can be identified. The first is the anonymous Scandinavian *[Saga of Hrolf Kraki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hr%C3%B3lfs_saga_kraka "Hrólfs saga kraka")*. In this, the murdered king has two sons— [Hroar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hro%C3%B0gar "Hroðgar") and [Helgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halga "Halga") —who spend most of the story in disguise, under false names, rather than feigning madness, in a sequence of events that differs from Shakespeare's.[^20] The second is the Roman legend of [Brutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Junius_Brutus#Biography "Lucius Junius Brutus"), recorded in two separate Latin works. Its hero, Lucius ("shining, light"), changes his name and persona to Brutus ("dull, stupid"), playing the role of a fool to avoid the fate of his father and brothers, and eventually slaying his family's killer, [King Tarquinius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Tarquinius_Superbus "Lucius Tarquinius Superbus"). A 17th-century Nordic scholar, [Torfaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torfaeus "Torfaeus"), compared the Icelandic hero [Amlóði](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aml%C3%B3%C3%B0i "Amlóði") (Amlodi) and the hero Prince Ambales (from the *Ambales Saga*) to Shakespeare's *Hamlet*. Similarities include the prince's feigned madness, his accidental killing of the king's counsellor in his mother's bedroom, and the eventual slaying of his uncle.[^21]

Many of the earlier legendary elements are interwoven in the 13th-century "Life of Amleth" (Latin: *Vita Amlethi*) by [Saxo Grammaticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxo_Grammaticus "Saxo Grammaticus"), part of *[Gesta Danorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesta_Danorum "Gesta Danorum")*.[^22] Written in Latin, it reflects classical Roman concepts of virtue and heroism, and was widely available in Shakespeare's day.[^23] Significant parallels include the prince feigning madness, his mother's hasty marriage to the usurper, the prince killing a hidden spy, and the prince substituting the execution of two retainers for his own. A reasonably faithful version of Saxo's story was translated into French in 1570 by [François de Belleforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_de_Belleforest "François de Belleforest"), in his *Histoires tragiques*.[^24] Belleforest embellished Saxo's text substantially, almost doubling its length, and introduced the hero's [melancholy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melancholia "Melancholia").[^25]

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_Spanish_Tragedy.jpg)

Title page of The Spanish Tragedy by Thomas Kyd

According to one theory, Shakespeare's main source may be an earlier play—now lost—known today as the *[Ur-Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ur-Hamlet "Ur-Hamlet")*. Possibly written by [Thomas Kyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kyd "Thomas Kyd") or by Shakespeare, the *Ur-Hamlet* would have existed by 1589, and would have incorporated a ghost.[^26] Shakespeare's company, [the Chamberlain's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain's_Men "Lord Chamberlain's Men"), may have purchased that play and performed a version for some time, which Shakespeare reworked.[^27] However, no copy of the *Ur-Hamlet* has survived, and it is impossible to compare its language and style with the known works of any of its putative authors. In 1936 [Andrew Cairncross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Cairncross "Andrew Cairncross") suggested that, until more becomes known, it may be assumed that Shakespeare wrote the *Ur-Hamlet*.[^28] [Eric Sams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Sams "Eric Sams") lists reasons for supporting Shakespeare's authorship.[^29] [Harold Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jenkins_\(Shakespeare_scholar\) "Harold Jenkins (Shakespeare scholar)") considers that there are no grounds for thinking that the *Ur-Hamlet* is an early work by Shakespeare, which he then rewrote.[^30] Professor Terri Bourus in 2016, one of three general editors of the New Oxford Shakespeare,[^31] in her paper "Enter Shakespeare's Young Hamlet, 1589" suggests that Shakespeare was "interested in sixteenth-century French literature, from the very beginning of his career" and therefore "did not need Thomas Kyd to pre-digest Belleforest's histoire of Amleth and spoon-feed it to him". She considers that the hypothesised *Ur-Hamlet* is Shakespeare's Q1 text, and that this derived directly from Belleforest's French version.[^32]

The precise combination of Shakespeare's use of the *Ur-Hamlet*, Belleforest, Saxo, or Kyd's *[The Spanish Tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spanish_Tragedy "The Spanish Tragedy")* as sources for *Hamlet* is not known. However, elements of Belleforest's version which are not in Saxo's story do appear in Shakespeare's play.[^33]

Most scholars reject the idea that *Hamlet* is in any way connected with Shakespeare's only son, [Hamnet Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamnet_Shakespeare "Hamnet Shakespeare"), who died in 1596 at age eleven. Conventional wisdom holds that *Hamlet* is strongly connected to legend, and the name Hamnet was quite popular at the time.[^34] However, [Stephen Greenblatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Greenblatt "Stephen Greenblatt") has argued that the [coincidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coincidence "Coincidence") of the names and Shakespeare's grief for the loss of his son may lie at the heart of the tragedy. He notes that the name of Hamnet Sadler, the Stratford neighbour after whom Hamnet was named, was often written as Hamlet Sadler and that, in the loose orthography of the time, the names were virtually interchangeable.[^35] [^36]

Scholars have often speculated that *Hamlet* 's [Polonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonius "Polonius") might have been inspired by [William Cecil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cecil,_1st_Baron_Burghley "William Cecil, 1st Baron Burghley") (Lord Burghley)—Lord High Treasurer and chief counsellor to Queen [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I "Elizabeth I"). [E. K. Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._K._Chambers "E. K. Chambers") suggested Polonius's advice to Laertes may have echoed Burghley's to his son [Robert Cecil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Cecil,_1st_Earl_of_Salisbury "Robert Cecil, 1st Earl of Salisbury").[^37] [John Dover Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dover_Wilson "John Dover Wilson") thought it almost certain that the figure of Polonius caricatured Burghley.[^38] [A. L. Rowse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._L._Rowse "A. L. Rowse") speculated that Polonius's tedious verbosity might have resembled Burghley's.[^39] Lilian Winstanley thought the name Corambis (in the First Quarto) did suggest Cecil and Burghley.[^40] [Harold Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jenkins_\(Shakespeare_scholar\) "Harold Jenkins (Shakespeare scholar)") considers the idea of Polonius as a caricature of Burghley to be conjecture, perhaps based on the similar role they each played at court, and perhaps also based on the similarity between Burghley addressing his *Ten Precepts* to his son, and Polonius offering "precepts" to his son, Laertes.[^41] Jenkins suggests that any personal satire may be found in the name "Polonius", which might point to a Polish or Polonian connection.[^42] G. R. Hibbard hypothesised that differences in names (Corambis/Polonius:Montano/Raynoldo) between the First Quarto and other editions might reflect a desire not to offend scholars at [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"). ([Robert Pullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Pullen "Robert Pullen"), was the founder of Oxford University, and [John Rainolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rainolds "John Rainolds"), was the President of [Corpus Christi College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Christi_College,_Oxford "Corpus Christi College, Oxford").) [^43]

## Date

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:John_Barrymore_Hamlet_1922.jpg)

John Barrymore as Hamlet (1922)

"Any dating of *Hamlet* must be tentative", states the *New Cambridge* editor, [Phillip Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Edwards_\(academic\) "Philip Edwards (academic)"). MacCary suggests 1599 or 1600;[^44] [James S. Shapiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_S._Shapiro "James S. Shapiro") offers late 1600 or early 1601;[^45] [Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Wells "Stanley Wells") and [Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Taylor_\(scholar\) "Gary Taylor (scholar)") suggest that the play was written in 1600 and revised later;[^46] the New Cambridge editor settles on mid-1601;[^47] the New Swan Shakespeare Advanced Series editor agrees with 1601;[^48] Thompson and Taylor, tentatively ("according to whether one is the more persuaded by Jenkins or by Honigmann") suggest a *[terminus ad quem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminus_post_quem "Terminus post quem")* of either spring 1601 or sometime in 1600.[^49]

The [earliest date estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminus_post_quem "Terminus post quem") relies on *Hamlet* 's frequent allusions to Shakespeare's *[Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar_\(play\) "Julius Caesar (play)")*, itself dated to mid-1599.[^50] [^51] The [latest date estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminus_post_quem "Terminus post quem") is based on an entry, of 26 July 1602, in the [Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationers'_Register "Stationers' Register") of the [Stationers' Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worshipful_Company_of_Stationers_and_Newspaper_Makers "Worshipful Company of Stationers and Newspaper Makers"), indicating that *Hamlet* was "latelie Acted by the [Lo: Chamberleyne his servantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain's_Men "Lord Chamberlain's Men") ".[^52]

In 1598, [Francis Meres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Meres "Francis Meres") published his *Palladis Tamia*, a survey of English literature from Chaucer to its present day, within which twelve of Shakespeare's plays are named. *Hamlet* is not among them, suggesting that it had not yet been written. As *Hamlet* was very popular, Bernard Lott, the series editor of *New Swan*, believes it "unlikely that he \[Meres\] would have overlooked... so significant a piece".[^48]

The phrase "little eyases" [^53] in the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio "First Folio") (F1) may allude to the [Children of the Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_the_Chapel "Children of the Chapel"), whose popularity in London forced the Globe company into provincial touring.[^54] This became known as the [War of the Theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Theatres "War of the Theatres"), and supports a 1601 dating.[^48] [Katherine Duncan-Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Duncan-Jones "Katherine Duncan-Jones") accepts a 1600–01 attribution for the date *Hamlet* was written, but notes that the [Lord Chamberlain's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain's_Men "Lord Chamberlain's Men"), playing *Hamlet* in the 3000-capacity [Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre "Globe Theatre"), were unlikely to be put to any disadvantage by an audience of "barely one hundred" for the Children of the chapel's equivalent play, *[Antonio's Revenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio's_Revenge "Antonio's Revenge")*; she believes that Shakespeare, confident in the superiority of his own work, was making a playful and charitable allusion to his friend [John Marston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Marston_\(playwright\) "John Marston (playwright)") 's very similar piece.[^55]

A contemporary of Shakespeare's, [Gabriel Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Harvey "Gabriel Harvey"), wrote a marginal note in his copy of the 1598 edition of [Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer "Geoffrey Chaucer") 's works, which some scholars use as dating evidence. Harvey's note says that "the wiser sort" enjoy *Hamlet*, and implies that [the Earl of Essex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Devereux,_2nd_Earl_of_Essex "Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex") —executed in February 1601 for rebellion—was still alive. Other scholars consider this inconclusive. Edwards, for example, concludes that the "sense of time is so confused in Harvey's note that it is really of little use in trying to date *Hamlet* ". This is because the same note also refers to [Spenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser "Edmund Spenser") and [Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Watson_\(poet\) "Thomas Watson (poet)") as if they were still alive ("our flourishing [metricians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter_\(poetry\) "Meter (poetry)") "), but also mentions " [Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Owen_\(epigrammatist\) "John Owen (epigrammatist)") 's new epigrams", published in 1607.[^56]

## Texts

Three early editions of the text, each different, have survived, making attempts to establish a single "authentic" text problematic.[^57] [^58] [^59]

- [First Quarto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_Q1 "Hamlet Q1") (**Q1**): In 1603 the booksellers [Nicholas Ling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Ling "Nicholas Ling") and John Trundell published, and [Valentine Simmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentine_Simmes "Valentine Simmes") printed, the so-called " [bad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_quarto "Bad quarto") " first quarto, under the name *The Tragicall Historie of Hamlet Prince of Denmarke*. Q1 contains just over half of the text of the later second quarto.
- Second Quarto (**Q2**): In 1604 Nicholas Ling published, and James Roberts printed, the second quarto, under the same name as the first. Some copies are dated 1605, which may indicate a second impression; consequently, Q2 is often dated "1604/5". Q2 is the longest early edition, although it omits about 77 lines found in F1 [^60] (most likely to avoid offending [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_I_of_England "James I of England") 's queen, [Anne of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_of_Denmark "Anne of Denmark")).[^61]
- [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio "First Folio") (**F1**): In 1623 [Edward Blount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Blount "Edward Blount") and [William and Isaac Jaggard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jaggard "William Jaggard") published *The Tragedie of Hamlet, Prince of Denmarke* in the First Folio, the first edition of Shakespeare's *Complete Works*.[^62]

This list does not include three additional early texts, [John Smethwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Smethwick "John Smethwick") 's Q3, Q4, and Q5 (1611–37), which are regarded as reprints of Q2 with some alterations.[^62]

Early [editors of Shakespeare's works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_editors "Shakespeare's editors"), beginning with [Nicholas Rowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Rowe_\(writer\) "Nicholas Rowe (writer)") (1709) and [Lewis Theobald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Theobald "Lewis Theobald") (1733), combined material from the two earliest sources of *Hamlet* available at the time, Q2 and F1. Each text contains material that the other lacks, with many minor differences in wording: scarcely 200 lines are identical in the two. Editors have combined them in an effort to create one "inclusive" text that reflects an imagined "ideal" of Shakespeare's original. Theobald's version became standard for a long time,[^63] and his "full text" approach continues to influence editorial practice to the present day. Some contemporary scholarship, however, discounts this approach, instead considering "an authentic *Hamlet* an unrealisable ideal.... there are *texts* of this play but no *text* ".[^64] The 2006 publication by Arden Shakespeare of different *Hamlet* texts in different volumes is perhaps evidence of this shifting focus and emphasis.[^1] Other editors have continued to argue the need for well-edited editions taking material from all versions of the play. Colin Burrow has argued that

> most of us should read a text that is made up by conflating all three versions... it's about as likely that Shakespeare wrote: "To be or not to be, ay, there's the point" \[in Q1\], as that he wrote the works of [Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon "Francis Bacon"). I suspect most people just won't want to read a three-text play... \[multi-text editions are\] a version of the play that is out of touch with the needs of a wider public.[^69]

Traditionally, editors of Shakespeare's plays have divided them into five acts. None of the early texts of *Hamlet*, however, were arranged this way, and the play's division into acts and scenes derives from a 1676 quarto. Modern editors generally follow this traditional division but consider it unsatisfactory; for example, after Hamlet drags Polonius's body out of Gertrude's bedchamber, there is an act-break [^70] after which the action appears to continue uninterrupted.[^71]

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bad_quarto,_good_quarto,_first_folio.png)

Comparison of the 'To be, or not to be' soliloquy in the first three editions of Hamlet, showing the varying quality of the text in the Bad Quarto, the Good Quarto and the First Folio

Q1 was discovered in 1823. Only two copies are extant. According to Jenkins, "The unauthorized nature of this quarto is matched by the corruption of its text." [^72] Yet Q1 has value: it contains stage directions (such as Ophelia entering with a lute and her hair down) that reveal actual stage practices in a way that Q2 and F1 do not; it contains an entire scene (usually labelled 4.6) [^73] that does not appear in either Q2 or F1; and it is useful for comparison with the later editions. The major deficiency of Q1 is in the language: particularly noticeable in the opening lines of the famous " [To be, or not to be](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_be,_or_not_to_be "To be, or not to be") " soliloquy: "To be, or not to be, aye there's the point. / To die, to sleep, is that all? Aye all: / No, to sleep, to dream, aye marry there it goes." However, the scene order is more coherent, without the problems of Q2 and F1 of Hamlet seeming to resolve something in one scene and enter the next drowning in indecision. New Cambridge editor Kathleen Irace has noted that "Q1's more linear plot design is certainly easier \[...\] to follow \[...\] but the simplicity of the Q1 plot arrangement eliminates the alternating plot elements that correspond to Hamlet's shifts in mood." [^74]

Q1 is considerably shorter than Q2 or F1 and may be a [memorial reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial_reconstruction "Memorial reconstruction") of the play as Shakespeare's company performed it, by an actor who played a minor role (most likely Marcellus).[^75] Scholars disagree whether the reconstruction was pirated or authorised. It is suggested by Irace that Q1 is an abridged version intended especially for travelling productions, thus the question of length may be considered as separate from issues of poor textual quality.[^68] [^76] Editing Q1 thus poses problems in whether or not to "correct" differences from Q2 and F. Irace, in her introduction to Q1, wrote that "I have avoided as many other alterations as possible, because the differences...are especially intriguing...I have recorded a selection of Q2/F readings in the collation." The idea that Q1 is not riddled with error but is instead eminently fit for the stage has led to at least 28 different Q1 productions since 1881.[^77] Other productions have used the Q2 and Folio texts, but used Q1's running order, in particular moving the *to be or not to be* soliloquy earlier.[^78] Developing this, some editors such as [Jonathan Bate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Bate "Jonathan Bate") have argued that Q2 may represent "a 'reading' text as opposed to a 'performance' one" of *Hamlet*: an edition containing all of Shakespeare's material for the play for the pleasure of readers, so not representing the play as it would have been staged.[^79] [^80]

## Analysis and criticism

### Critical history

From the early 17th century, the play was famous for its ghost and vivid dramatisation of [melancholy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melancholia "Melancholia") and [insanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insanity "Insanity"), leading to a procession of mad courtiers and ladies in [Jacobean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobean_era "Jacobean era") and [Caroline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_era "Caroline era") drama.[^81] [^82] Though it remained popular with mass audiences, late 17th-century [Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Restoration "English Restoration") critics saw *Hamlet* as primitive and disapproved of its lack of [unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_unities "Classical unities") and [decorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorum "Decorum").[^83] [^84] This view changed drastically in the 18th century, when critics regarded Hamlet as a hero—a pure, brilliant young man thrust into unfortunate circumstances.[^85]

By the mid-18th century, however, the advent of [Gothic literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_fiction "Gothic fiction") brought [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology") and [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism") readings, returning madness and the ghost to the forefront.[^86] Not until the late 18th century did critics and performers begin to view Hamlet as confusing and inconsistent. Before then, he was either mad, or not; either a hero, or not; with no in-betweens.[^87] These developments represented a fundamental change in literary criticism, which came to focus more on character and less on plot.[^88] The French philosopher [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire "Voltaire") published a harsh criticism of *Hamlet*, which offended his [neoclassical principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassicism "Neoclassicism"). He described it as a "vulgar and barbarous drama" and particularly took exception to its gravedigger scene, although he also conceded that it contains "some sublime passages, worthy of the greatest genius".[^89]

By the 19th century, [Romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") critics valued *Hamlet* for its internal, individual conflict reflecting the strong contemporary emphasis on internal struggles and inner character in general.[^90] Then too, critics started to focus on Hamlet's delay as a character trait, rather than a plot device. This focus on character and internal struggle continued into the 20th century;[^91] additional perspectives can be found in [context and interpretation](#Context_and_interpretation) below.

### Dramatic structure

Modern editors have divided the play into five acts, and each act into scenes. The First Folio marks the first two acts only. The quartos do not have such divisions. The division into five acts follows [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger "Seneca the Younger"), who in his plays, regularised the way ancient Greek tragedies contain five episodes, which are separated by four choral odes. In *Hamlet* the development of the plot or the action are determined by the unfolding of Hamlet's character. The soliloquies do not interrupt the plot, instead they are highlights of each block of action. The plot is the developing revelation of Hamlet's view of what is "rotten in the state of Denmark". The action of the play is driven forward in dialogue; but in the soliloquies time and action stop, the meaning of action is questioned, fog of illusion is broached, and truths are exposed.[^92]

The contrast between appearance and reality is a significant theme. Hamlet is presented with an image, and then interprets its deeper or darker meaning. Examples begin with Hamlet questioning the reality of the ghost. It continues with Hamlet's taking on an "antic disposition" in order to appear mad, though he is not. The contrast (appearance and reality) is also expressed in several "spying scenes": Act two begins with Polonius sending Reynaldo to spy on his son, Laertes. Claudius and Polonius spy on Ophelia as she meets with Hamlet. In act two, Claudius asks Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to spy on Hamlet. Similarly, the play-within-a-play is used by Hamlet to reveal his step-father's hidden nature.[^92]

There is no subplot, but the play presents the affairs of the courtier Polonius, his daughter, Ophelia, and his son, Laertes—who variously deal with madness, love and the death of a father in ways that contrast with Hamlet's. The graveyard scene eases tension prior to the catastrophe, and, as Hamlet holds the skull, it is shown that Hamlet no longer fears damnation in the afterlife, and accepts that there is a "divinity that shapes our ends".[^92]

Hamlet's enquiring mind has been open to all kinds of ideas, but in act five he has decided on a plan, and in a dialogue with Horatio he seems to answer his two earlier soliloquies on suicide: "We defy augury. There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow. If it be now, 'tis not to come; if it be not to come, it will be now; if it be not now, yet it will come. The readiness is all. Since no man, of aught he leaves, knows aught, what is't to leave betimes." [^93] [^92]

### Length

The First Quarto (1603) text of *Hamlet* contains 15,983 words, the Second Quarto (1604) contains 28,628 words, and the First Folio (1623) contains 27,602 words. Counting the number of lines varies between editions, partly because prose sections in the play may be formatted with varied lengths.[^94] Editions of *Hamlet* that are created by conflating the texts of the Second Quarto and the Folio are said to have approximately 3,900 lines;[^95] the number of lines varies between those editions based on formatting the prose sections, counting methods, and how the editors have joined the texts together.[^96] *Hamlet* is by far the longest play that Shakespeare wrote, and one of the longest plays in the [Western canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_canon "Western canon"). It might require more than four hours to stage;[^97] a typical Elizabethan play would need two to three hours.[^98] It is speculated that because of the considerable length of Q2 and F1, there was an expectation that those texts would be abridged for performance, or that Q2 and F1 may have been aimed at a reading audience.[^99]

That Q1 is so much shorter than Q2 has spurred speculation that Q1 is an early draft, or perhaps an adaptation, a bootleg copy, or a stage adaptation. On the title page of Q2, its text is described as "newly imprinted and enlarged to almost as much again as it was". That is probably a comparison to Q1.[^94]

### Language

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/ba/Delacroix-1834-I2-QueenConsolesHamlet.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)

Hamlet's statement that his dark clothes are the outer sign of his inner grief demonstrates strong rhetorical skill (artist: Eugène Delacroix 1834).

Much of *Hamlet* 's language is courtly: elaborate, witty discourse, as recommended by [Baldassare Castiglione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldassare_Castiglione "Baldassare Castiglione") 's 1528 etiquette guide, *The Courtier*. This work specifically advises royal retainers to amuse their masters with inventive language. Osric and Polonius, especially, seem to respect this injunction. Claudius's speech is rich with rhetorical figures—as is Hamlet's and, at times, Ophelia's—while the language of Horatio, the guards, and the gravediggers is simpler. Claudius's high status is reinforced by using the [royal first person plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majestic_plural "Majestic plural") ("we" or "us"), and [anaphora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaphora_\(rhetoric\) "Anaphora (rhetoric)") mixed with [metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor "Metaphor") to resonate with Greek political speeches.[^100]

Of all the characters, Hamlet has the greatest rhetorical skill. He uses highly developed metaphors, [stichomythia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stichomythia "Stichomythia"), and in nine memorable words deploys both anaphora and [asyndeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asyndeton "Asyndeton"): "to die: to sleep— / To sleep, perchance to dream".[^101] In contrast, when occasion demands, he is precise and straightforward, as when he explains his inward emotion to his mother: "But I have that within which passes show, / These but the trappings and the suits of woe".[^102] At times, he relies heavily on [puns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pun "Pun") to express his true thoughts while simultaneously concealing them.[^103] Pauline Kiernan argues that Shakespeare changed English drama forever in *Hamlet* because he "showed how a character's language can often be saying several things at once, and contradictory meanings at that, to reflect fragmented thoughts and disturbed feelings". She gives the example of Hamlet's advice to Ophelia, "get thee to a nunnery",[^104] which, she claims, is simultaneously a reference to a place of chastity and a slang term for a brothel, reflecting Hamlet's confused feelings about female sexuality.[^105] However Harold Jenkins does not agree, having studied the few examples that are used to support that idea, and finds that there is no support for the assumption that "nunnery" was used that way in slang, or that Hamlet intended such a meaning. The context of the scene suggests that a nunnery would not be a brothel, but instead a place of renunciation and a "sanctuary from marriage and from the world's contamination".[^106] Thompson and Taylor consider the brothel idea incorrect considering that "Hamlet is trying to deter Ophelia from *breeding* ".[^107]

Hamlet's first words in the play are a pun; when Claudius addresses him as "my cousin Hamlet, and my son", Hamlet says as an aside: "A little more than kin, and less than kind." [^108]

An unusual rhetorical device, [hendiadys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendiadys "Hendiadys"), appears in several places in the play. Examples are found in Ophelia's speech at the end of the nunnery scene: "Th' *expectancy and rose* of the fair state" [^109] and "And I, of ladies most *deject and wretched* ".[^110] Many scholars have found it odd that Shakespeare would, seemingly arbitrarily, use this rhetorical form throughout the play. One explanation may be that *Hamlet* was written later in Shakespeare's life, when he was adept at matching rhetorical devices to characters and the plot. Linguist George T. Wright suggests that hendiadys had been used deliberately to heighten the play's sense of duality and dislocation.[^111]

Hamlet's [soliloquies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soliloquy "Soliloquy") have captured the attention of scholars. Hamlet interrupts himself, vocalising either disgust or agreement with himself and embellishing his own words. He has difficulty expressing himself directly and instead blunts the thrust of his thought with wordplay. It is not until late in the play, after his experience with the pirates, that Hamlet is able to articulate his feelings freely.[^112]

## Context and interpretation

### Religious

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:John_Everett_Millais_-_Ophelia_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

John Everett Millais 's Ophelia (1852) depicts Lady Ophelia's mysterious death by drowning. In the play, the gravediggers discuss whether Ophelia's death was a suicide and whether she merits a Christian burial.

Written at a time of religious upheaval and in the wake of the [English Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation "English Reformation"), the play is alternately [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism "Catholicism") (or piously medieval) and [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism "Protestantism") (or consciously modern). The ghost describes himself as being in [purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory "Purgatory") and as dying without [last rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_rites "Last rites"). This and Ophelia's burial ceremony, which is characteristically Catholic, make up most of the play's Catholic connections. Some scholars have observed that [revenge tragedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenge_play "Revenge play") come from Catholic countries such as Italy and Spain, where the revenge tragedies present contradictions of motives, since according to Catholic doctrine the duty to God and family precedes civil justice.[^113]

Much of the play's Protestant tones derive from its setting in Denmark—both then and now a predominantly Protestant country,[^2] though it is unclear whether the fictional Denmark of the play is intended to portray this implicit fact. Dialogue refers explicitly to the German city of [Wittenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittenberg "Wittenberg") where Hamlet, Horatio, and Rosencrantz and Guildenstern attend university, implying where the Protestant [reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation "Reformation") [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther "Martin Luther") nailed the *[Ninety-five Theses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninety-five_Theses "Ninety-five Theses")* to the church door in 1517.[^114]

### Philosophical

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Michel_de_Montaigne_1.jpg)

Philosophical ideas in Hamlet are similar to those of the French writer Michel de Montaigne, a contemporary of Shakespeare's (artist: Thomas de Leu, fl. 1560–1612).

Hamlet is often perceived as a philosophical character, expounding ideas that are now described as [relativist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativism "Relativism"), [existentialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism"), and [sceptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scepticism "Scepticism"). For example, he expresses a subjectivistic idea when he says to Rosencrantz: "there is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so".[^115] The idea that nothing is real except in the mind of the individual finds its roots in the Greek [Sophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophism "Sophism"), who argued that since nothing can be perceived except through the senses—and since all individuals sense, and therefore perceive things differently—there is no absolute truth, but rather only relative truth.[^116] The clearest alleged instance of existentialism is in the " [to be, or not to be](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_be,_or_not_to_be "To be, or not to be") " [^117] speech.[^116]

*Hamlet* reflects the contemporary [scepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_scepticism "Philosophical scepticism") promoted by the French [Renaissance humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism "Renaissance humanism") [Michel de Montaigne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_de_Montaigne "Michel de Montaigne").[^118] Prior to Montaigne's time, humanists such as [Pico della Mirandola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pico_della_Mirandola "Pico della Mirandola") had argued that man was God's greatest creation, made in God's image and able to choose his own nature, but this view was subsequently challenged in Montaigne's *[Essais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_\(Montaigne\) "Essays (Montaigne)")* of 1580. Hamlet's " [What a piece of work is a man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_a_piece_of_work_is_a_man "What a piece of work is a man") " seems to echo many of Montaigne's ideas, and many scholars have discussed whether Shakespeare drew directly from Montaigne or whether both men were simply reacting similarly to the spirit of the times.[^119] [^120] [^118]

### Psychoanalytic

> "How if Hamlet had in years gone by, as a child, bitterly resented having had to share in his mother's affection even with his own father, had regarded him as a rival, and had secretly wished him out of the way so that he might enjoy undisputed and undisturbed the monopoly of that affection?" When Hamlet's father died, he could not but feel guilty that his wish had been granted, so his grieving was contaminated by guilt and a manufactured idolization of the missing parent.

— Ann Thompson and Neil Taylor, in turn quoting  
[Ernest Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Jones_\(psychoanalyst\) "Ernest Jones (psychoanalyst)") ' 1949 *[Hamlet and Oedipus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_and_Oedipus "Hamlet and Oedipus")*.[^121]

*Hamlet* has featured in some of the key texts of [psychoanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalysis "Psychoanalysis"), and the characters of Hamlet and Ophelia have become iconic representations of, respectively, male and female instability.[^122]

[Gaston Bachelard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_Bachelard "Gaston Bachelard") 's concept of the [Ophelia complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophelia_complex "Ophelia complex") related to the symbolic connections between women, water and death, which particularly merge in a representation of a drowning.[^122] [Elaine Showalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaine_Showalter "Elaine Showalter") saw Ophelia as a potent archetype of the connection between female insanity and sexuality: that men may go mad for any number of reasons; but that female madness is relentlessly regarded as related to their bodies and sexual desires.[^123]

[Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud") developed his ideas about the [Oedipus complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus_complex "Oedipus complex"), later expostulated in his *[The Interpretation of Dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Interpretation_of_Dreams "The Interpretation of Dreams")*, from analysing [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles "Sophocles") ' *[Oedipus Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus_Rex "Oedipus Rex")* and Shakespeare's *Hamlet*. He concluded that "Shakespeare's unconscious understood the unconscious of his hero." [^3] [^124] Freud's ideas about *Hamlet* were later expanded and adapted by [Ernest Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Jones_\(psychoanalyst\) "Ernest Jones (psychoanalyst)"), and directly influenced some twentieth century productions, including [Tyrone Guthrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrone_Guthrie "Tyrone Guthrie") 's 1937 *Hamlet* starring [Laurence Olivier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Olivier "Laurence Olivier").[^121] [^125]

[Jacques Lacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Lacan "Jacques Lacan") departed from Freud in his *Desire and the interpretation of desire in Hamlet*, in which he perceived the Ghost as 'the missing signifier, the veiled [phallus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phallus#Psychoanalysis "Phallus") '.[^4] And more recently Tom MacFaul's *Problem Fathers in Shakespeare and Renaissance Drama* explored the possibility that Hamlet's anxiety stems from a fear that Claudius, and not Old Hamlet, was his real father.[^127]

### Feminist

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ophelia_-_Henrietta_Rae.jpg)

Ophelia is distracted by grief. 128 Feminist critics have explored her descent into madness (artist: Henrietta Rae 1890).

In the 20th century, [feminist critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist_literary_criticism "Feminist literary criticism") opened up new approaches to Gertrude and Ophelia. [New historicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_historicism "New historicism") and [cultural materialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_materialism_\(cultural_studies\) "Cultural materialism (cultural studies)") critics examined the play in its historical context, attempting to piece together its original cultural environment.[^129] They focused on the [gender system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_role "Gender role") of [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period "Early modern period") England, pointing to the common trinity of *maid, wife, or widow*, with *whores* outside of that stereotype. In this analysis, the essence of *Hamlet* is the central character's changed perception of his mother as a whore because of her failure to remain faithful to Old Hamlet. In consequence, Hamlet loses his faith in all women, treating Ophelia as if she too were a whore and dishonest with Hamlet.[^130]

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Nicolai_Abildgaard_-_Hamlet_hos_sin_moder_-_KMS1019_-_Statens_Museum_for_Kunst.jpg)

Hamlet tries to show his mother Gertrude his father's ghost (artist: Nicolai A. Abildgaard, c. 1778 ).

[Carolyn Heilbrun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolyn_Heilbrun "Carolyn Heilbrun") 's 1957 essay "The Character of Hamlet's Mother" defends Gertrude, arguing that the text never hints that Gertrude knew of Claudius poisoning King Hamlet. This analysis has been praised by many feminist critics, combating what is, by Heilbrun's argument, centuries' worth of misinterpretation. By this account, Gertrude's worst crime is of pragmatically marrying her brother-in-law in order to avoid a power vacuum. This is borne out by the fact that King Hamlet's ghost tells Hamlet to leave Gertrude out of Hamlet's revenge, to leave her to heaven, an arbitrary mercy to grant to a conspirator to murder.[^131] [^132] [^133]

Ophelia has also been defended by feminist critics, most notably [Elaine Showalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaine_Showalter "Elaine Showalter").[^134] Ophelia is surrounded by powerful men: her father, brother, and Hamlet. All three disappear: Laertes leaves, Hamlet abandons her, and Polonius dies. Conventional theories had argued that without these three powerful men making decisions for her, Ophelia is driven into madness.[^135] Feminist theorists argue that she goes mad with guilt because, when Hamlet kills her father, he has fulfilled her sexual desire to have Hamlet kill her father so they can be together. Showalter points out that Ophelia has become the symbol of the distraught and hysterical woman in modern culture.[^136]

## Influence

*Hamlet* is one of the [most quoted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrases_from_Hamlet_in_common_English "Phrases from Hamlet in common English") works in the English language, and is often included on lists of the world's greatest literature.[^5] As such, it reverberates through the writing of later centuries. Academic Laurie Osborne identifies the direct influence of *Hamlet* in numerous modern narratives, and divides them into four main categories: fictional accounts of the play's composition, simplifications of the story for young readers, stories expanding the role of one or more characters, and narratives featuring performances of the play.[^138]

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Czach%C3%B3rski_Actors_before_Hamlet.jpg)

Actors before Hamlet by Władysław Czachórski (1875), National Museum in Warsaw

English poet [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton "John Milton") was an early admirer of Shakespeare and took evident inspiration from his work. As John Kerrigan discusses, Milton originally considered writing his epic poem *[Paradise Lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost "Paradise Lost")* (1667) as a tragedy.[^139] While Milton did not ultimately go that route, the poem still shows distinct echoes of Shakespearean revenge tragedy, and of *Hamlet* in particular. As scholar Christopher N. Warren argues, *Paradise Lost* 's Satan "undergoes a transformation in the poem from a Hamlet-like avenger into a Claudius-like usurper," a plot device that supports Milton's larger [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republicanism "Republicanism") internationalist project.[^140] The poem also reworks theatrical language from *Hamlet*, especially around the idea of "putting on" certain dispositions, as when Hamlet puts on "an antic disposition," similarly to the Son in *Paradise Lost* who "can put on / \[God's\] terrors." [^141]

[Henry Fielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Fielding "Henry Fielding") 's *[Tom Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_History_of_Tom_Jones,_a_Foundling "The History of Tom Jones, a Foundling")*, published about 1749, describes a visit to *Hamlet* by Tom Jones and Mr Partridge, with similarities to the "play within a play".[^142] In contrast, [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe") 's [Bildungsroman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bildungsroman "Bildungsroman") *[Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Meister's_Apprenticeship "Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship")*, written between 1776 and 1796, not only has a production of *Hamlet* at its core but also creates parallels between the ghost and Wilhelm Meister's dead father.[^142] In the early 1850s, in *[Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre;_or,_The_Ambiguities "Pierre; or, The Ambiguities")*, [Herman Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Melville "Herman Melville") focuses on a Hamlet-like character's long development as a writer.[^142] Ten years later, [Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens "Charles Dickens") 's *[Great Expectations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Expectations "Great Expectations")* contains many *Hamlet* -like plot elements: it is driven by revenge-motivated actions, contains ghost-like characters (Abel Magwitch and [Miss Havisham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Havisham "Miss Havisham")), and focuses on the hero's guilt.[^142] Academic Alexander Welsh notes that *Great Expectations* is an "autobiographical novel" and "anticipates psychoanalytic readings of *Hamlet* itself".[^143] About the same time, [George Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Eliot "George Eliot") 's *[The Mill on the Floss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mill_on_the_Floss "The Mill on the Floss")* was published, introducing Maggie Tulliver "who is explicitly compared with Hamlet" [^144] though "with a reputation for sanity".[^145]

In the 1920s, [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce") managed "a more upbeat version" of *Hamlet* —stripped of obsession and revenge—in *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(novel\) "Ulysses (novel)")*, though its main parallels are with [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer") 's *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey "Odyssey")*.[^142] In the 1990s, two novelists were explicitly influenced by *Hamlet*. In [Angela Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Carter "Angela Carter") 's *[Wise Children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wise_Children "Wise Children")*, *To be or not to be* is reworked as a song and dance routine, and [Iris Murdoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iris_Murdoch "Iris Murdoch") 's *[The Black Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Prince_\(novel\) "The Black Prince (novel)")* has Oedipal themes and murder intertwined with a love affair between a *Hamlet* -obsessed writer, Bradley Pearson, and the daughter of his rival.[^144] In the late 20th century, [David Foster Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Foster_Wallace "David Foster Wallace") 's novel *[Infinite Jest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_Jest "Infinite Jest")* draws from *Hamlet* and takes its title from the play's text.[^146] [^147]

## Performance history

> The day we see [Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet "Prince Hamlet") die in the theatre, something of him dies for us. He is dethroned by the spectre of an actor, and we shall never be able to keep the usurper out of our dreams.

—  [Maurice Maeterlinck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Maeterlinck "Maurice Maeterlinck") in *[La Jeune Belgique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Jeune_Belgique "La Jeune Belgique")* (1890).[^149]

### Shakespeare's day to the Interregnum

Shakespeare almost certainly wrote the role of Hamlet for [Richard Burbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Burbage "Richard Burbage"). He was the chief tragedian of the [Lord Chamberlain's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain's_Men "Lord Chamberlain's Men"), with a capacious memory for lines and a wide emotional range.[^150] [^151] [^6] Judging by the number of reprints, *Hamlet* appears to have been Shakespeare's fourth most popular play during his lifetime—only *[Henry IV Part 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV,_Part_1 "Henry IV, Part 1")*, *[Richard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_\(play\) "Richard III (play)")* and *[Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles,_Prince_of_Tyre "Pericles, Prince of Tyre")* eclipsed it.[^155] Shakespeare provides no clear indication of when his play is set; however, as Elizabethan actors performed at the [Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre "Globe Theatre") in contemporary dress on minimal sets, this would not have affected the staging.[^156]

Firm evidence for specific early performances of the play is scant. It is sometimes argued that the crew of the ship *[Red Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Dragon_\(1595\) "Red Dragon (1595)")*, anchored off [Sierra Leone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Leone "Sierra Leone"), performed *Hamlet* in September 1607;[^157] [^158] [^159] however, this claim is based on a 19th-century insert of a 'lost' passage into a period document, and is today widely regarded as a hoax, likely to have been perpetrated by [John Payne Collier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Payne_Collier "John Payne Collier").[^160] More credible is that the play toured in Germany within five years of Shakespeare's death,[^159] and that it was performed before [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_I_of_England "James I of England") in 1619 and [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England "Charles I of England") in 1637.[^161] Oxford editor George Hibbard argues that, since the contemporary literature contains many allusions and references to *Hamlet* (only [Falstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falstaff "Falstaff") is mentioned more, from Shakespeare), the play was surely performed with a frequency that the historical record misses.[^162]

All theatres were closed down by the [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan "Puritan") government during the [Interregnum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Interregnum "English Interregnum").[^163] Even during this time, however, playlets known as *[drolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droll "Droll")* were often performed illegally, including one called *The Grave-Makers* based on act 5, scene 1 of *Hamlet*.[^164]

### Restoration and 18th century

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hamlet,_Drury_Lane_Edition.jpg)

Title page and frontispiece for Hamlet, Prince of Denmark: A Tragedy. As it is now acted at the Theatres-Royal in Drury-Lane and Covent-Garden. London, 1776

The play was revived early in the [Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Restoration "English Restoration"). When the existing stock of pre- [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War "English Civil War") plays was divided between the two newly created [patent theatre companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent_theatre "Patent theatre"), *Hamlet* was the only Shakespearean favourite that [Sir William Davenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Davenant "William Davenant") 's [Duke's Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke's_Company "Duke's Company") secured.[^165] It became the first of Shakespeare's plays to be presented with movable [flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flats_\(theatre\) "Flats (theatre)") painted with generic scenery behind the [proscenium arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proscenium "Proscenium") of [Lincoln's Inn Fields Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln's_Inn_Fields "Lincoln's Inn Fields").[^7] This new stage convention highlighted the frequency with which Shakespeare shifts dramatic location, encouraging the recurrent criticism of his failure to maintain [unity of place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_unities "Classical unities").[^167] In the title role, Davenant cast [Thomas Betterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Betterton "Thomas Betterton"), who continued to play the Dane until he was 74.[^168] [David Garrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Garrick "David Garrick") at [Drury Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Drury_Lane "Theatre Royal, Drury Lane") produced a version that adapted Shakespeare heavily; he declared: "I had sworn I would not leave the stage till I had rescued that noble play from all the rubbish of the fifth act. I have brought it forth without the grave-digger's trick, Osrick, & the fencing match".[^8] The first actor known to have played Hamlet in North America is Lewis Hallam Jr., in the [American Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Company "American Company") 's production in Philadelphia in 1759.[^170]

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Garrick_as_Hamlet.jpg)

David Garrick expresses Hamlet's shock at his first sighting of the ghost (artist: unknown).

[John Philip Kemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philip_Kemble "John Philip Kemble") made his Drury Lane debut as Hamlet in 1783.[^171] His performance was said to be 20 minutes longer than anyone else's, and his lengthy pauses provoked the suggestion by [Richard Brinsley Sheridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brinsley_Sheridan "Richard Brinsley Sheridan") that "music should be played between the words".[^172] [Sarah Siddons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Siddons "Sarah Siddons") was the first actress known to play Hamlet; many women have since played him as a [breeches role](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeches_role "Breeches role"), to great acclaim.[^173] In 1748, [Alexander Sumarokov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Sumarokov "Alexander Sumarokov") wrote a Russian adaptation that focused on Prince Hamlet as the embodiment of an opposition to Claudius's tyranny—a treatment that would recur in Eastern European versions into the 20th century.[^174] In the years following America's independence, [Thomas Abthorpe Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Abthorpe_Cooper "Thomas Abthorpe Cooper"), the young nation's leading tragedian, performed *Hamlet* among other plays at the Chestnut Street Theatre in Philadelphia, and at the [Park Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Theatre_\(Manhattan\) "Park Theatre (Manhattan)") in New York. Although chided for "acknowledging acquaintances in the audience" and "inadequate memorisation of his lines", he became a national celebrity.[^175]

### 19th century

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thos._W._Keene_in_Hamlet.png)

A poster, c. 1884, for an American production of Hamlet (starring Thomas W. Keene ), showing several of the key scenes

From around 1810 to 1840, the best-known Shakespearean performances in the United States were tours by leading London actors—including [George Frederick Cooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Frederick_Cooke "George Frederick Cooke"), [Junius Brutus Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junius_Brutus_Booth "Junius Brutus Booth"), [Edmund Kean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Kean "Edmund Kean"), [William Charles Macready](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Charles_Macready "William Charles Macready"), and [Charles Kemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Kemble "Charles Kemble"). Of these, Booth remained to make his career in the States, fathering the nation's most notorious actor, [John Wilkes Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkes_Booth "John Wilkes Booth") (who later assassinated [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln "Abraham Lincoln")), and its most famous Hamlet, [Edwin Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Booth "Edwin Booth").[^176] Edwin Booth's *Hamlet* at the [Fifth Avenue Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Avenue_Theatre "Fifth Avenue Theatre") in 1875 was described as "... the dark, sad, dreamy, mysterious hero of a poem. \[... acted\] in an ideal manner, as far removed as possible from the plane of actual life".[^177] [^178] Booth played Hamlet for 100 nights in the 1864/5 season at the [Winter Garden Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Garden_Theatre_\(1850\) "Winter Garden Theatre (1850)"), inaugurating the era of long-run Shakespeare in America.[^178]

In the United Kingdom, the actor-managers of the [Victorian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era "Victorian era") (including Kean, [Samuel Phelps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Phelps "Samuel Phelps"), Macready, and [Henry Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Irving "Henry Irving")) staged Shakespeare in a grand manner, with elaborate scenery and costumes.[^179] The tendency of actor-managers to emphasise the importance of their own central character did not always meet with the critics' approval. [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw "George Bernard Shaw") 's praise for [Johnston Forbes-Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnston_Forbes-Robertson "Johnston Forbes-Robertson") 's performance contains a sideswipe at Irving: "The story of the play was perfectly intelligible, and quite took the attention of the audience off the principal actor at moments. What is the [Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum_Theatre,_London "Lyceum Theatre, London") coming to?" [^9]

In London, Edmund Kean was the first Hamlet to abandon the regal finery usually associated with the role in favour of a plain costume, and he is said to have surprised his audience by playing Hamlet as serious and introspective.[^181] In stark contrast to earlier opulence, [William Poel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Poel "William Poel") 's 1881 production of the Q1 text was an early attempt at reconstructing the Elizabethan theatre's austerity; his only backdrop was a set of red curtains.[^61] [^182] [Sarah Bernhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Bernhardt "Sarah Bernhardt") played the prince in her popular 1899 London production. In contrast to the "effeminate" view of the central character that usually accompanied a female casting, she described her character as "manly and resolute, but nonetheless thoughtful... \[he\] thinks before he acts, a trait indicative of great strength and great spiritual power".[^10]

In France, Charles Kemble initiated an enthusiasm for Shakespeare; and leading members of the Romantic movement such as [Victor Hugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hugo "Victor Hugo") and [Alexandre Dumas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Dumas "Alexandre Dumas") saw his 1827 Paris performance of *Hamlet*, particularly admiring the madness of [Harriet Smithson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Smithson "Harriet Smithson") 's Ophelia.[^184] In Germany, *Hamlet* had become so assimilated by the mid-19th century that [Ferdinand Freiligrath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Freiligrath "Ferdinand Freiligrath") declared that "Germany is Hamlet".[^185] From the 1850s, the [Parsi theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsi_theatre "Parsi theatre") tradition in India transformed *Hamlet* into folk performances, with dozens of songs added.[^186]

### 20th century

Apart from some western troupes' 19th-century visits, the first professional performance of Hamlet in Japan was [Otojirō Kawakami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otojir%C5%8D_Kawakami "Otojirō Kawakami") 's 1903 *[Shinpa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimpa "Shimpa")* ("new school theatre") adaptation.[^187] [Tsubouchi Shōyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsubouchi_Sh%C5%8Dy%C5%8D "Tsubouchi Shōyō") translated *Hamlet* and produced a staging in 1911 that blended *Shingeki* ("new drama") and *[Kabuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabuki "Kabuki")* styles.[^187] This hybrid-genre reached its peak in [Tsuneari Fukuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuneari_Fukuda "Tsuneari Fukuda") 's 1955 *Hamlet*.[^187] In 1998, [Yukio Ninagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukio_Ninagawa "Yukio Ninagawa") produced an acclaimed version in the style of [Nō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noh "Noh") theatre, which he took to London.[^188]

[Konstantin Stanislavski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Stanislavski "Konstantin Stanislavski") and [Edward Gordon Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Gordon_Craig "Edward Gordon Craig") collaborated on the [Moscow Art Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Art_Theatre "Moscow Art Theatre") 's [production of 1911–12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Art_Theatre_production_of_Hamlet "Moscow Art Theatre production of Hamlet").[^11] While Craig favoured stylised abstraction, Stanislavski, armed with his ['system,'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislavski's_'system "Stanislavski's 'system'") explored psychological motivation.[^190] Craig conceived of the play as a [symbolist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_\(arts\) "Symbolism (arts)") [monodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodrama "Monodrama"), offering a dream-like vision as seen through Hamlet's eyes alone.[^12] This was most evident in the staging of the first court scene.[^194] [^13] The most famous aspect of the production is Craig's use of large, abstract screens that altered the size and shape of the acting area for each scene, representing the character's state of mind spatially or visualising a [dramaturgical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgy "Dramaturgy") progression.[^196] The production attracted enthusiastic and unprecedented worldwide attention for the theatre and placed it "on the cultural map for Western Europe".[^197] [^198]

The first modern dress stagings of *Hamlet* occurred in 1925 in London and then New York. Barry Jackson's [Birmingham Repertory Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Repertory_Theatre "Birmingham Repertory Theatre") opened their production, directed by H.K. Ayliff at the Kingsway Theatre on August 25, 1925.[^199] Ivor Brown reported, "Many of the first night audience came to scoff and remained to hold its breath, to marvel and enjoy... Shakespeare's victory over time and tailoring was swift and sweeping." [^200] Horace Brisbin Liveright's modern dress production opened in New York on November 9, 1925, the same night the London production moved to Birmingham. It was known "more dryly, and perhaps with a touch of something more sinister, as 'the plain-clothes *Hamlet* '" and did not reach the same level of success.[^199]

*Hamlet* is often played with contemporary political overtones. [Leopold Jessner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Jessner "Leopold Jessner") 's 1926 production at the Berlin Staatstheater portrayed Claudius's court as a parody of the corrupt and fawning court of [Kaiser Wilhelm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_II,_German_Emperor "William II, German Emperor").[^201] In [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland"), the number of productions of *Hamlet* has tended to increase at times of political unrest, since its political themes (suspected crimes, coups, surveillance) can be used to comment on a contemporary situation.[^202] Similarly, [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic "Czech Republic") directors have used the play at times of occupation: a 1941 [Vinohrady Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinohrady "Vinohrady") production "emphasised, with due caution, the helpless situation of an intellectual attempting to endure in a ruthless environment".[^203] [^204] In China, performances of Hamlet often have political significance: Gu Wuwei's 1916 *The Usurper of State Power*, an amalgam of *Hamlet* and *Macbeth*, was an attack on [Yuan Shikai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_Shikai "Yuan Shikai") 's attempt to overthrow the republic.[^205] In 1942, [Jiao Juyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiao_Juyin "Jiao Juyin") directed the play in a [Confucian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism "Confucianism") temple in [Sichuan Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sichuan "Sichuan"), to which the government had retreated from the advancing Japanese.[^205] In the immediate aftermath of the collapse of the [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiananmen_Square_protests_of_1989 "Tiananmen Square protests of 1989") at [Tiananmen Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiananmen_Square "Tiananmen Square"), Lin Zhaohua staged a 1990 *Hamlet* in which the prince was an ordinary individual tortured by a loss of meaning.[^206]

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mignon_Nevada_Ophelia2.jpg)

Mignon Nevada as Ophelia, 1910

High-profile stagings in London and New York included [John Barrymore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Barrymore "John Barrymore") 's 1925 production at the [Haymarket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_Theatre "Haymarket Theatre"), which influenced subsequent performances by [John Gielgud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gielgud "John Gielgud") and [Laurence Olivier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Olivier "Laurence Olivier").[^207] [^208] Gielgud played the central role many times: his 1936 New York production ran for 132 performances, leading to the accolade that he was "the finest interpreter of the role since Barrymore".[^209] Although "posterity has treated [Maurice Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Evans_\(actor\) "Maurice Evans (actor)") less kindly", during the 1930s and 1940s he was regarded by many as the leading interpreter of Shakespeare in the United States, and in the 1938/39 season he presented [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre "Broadway theatre") 's first uncut *Hamlet*, running four and a half hours.[^210] Evans later performed a highly truncated version of the play that he played for South Pacific war zones during World War II which made the prince a more decisive character. The staging, known as the "G.I. Hamlet", was produced on Broadway for 131 performances in 1945/46.[^211]

Olivier's 1937 performance at [The Old Vic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Vic "The Old Vic") was popular with audiences but not with all critics; [James Agate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Agate "James Agate") wrote in *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times "The Sunday Times"),* "Mr. Olivier does not speak poetry badly. He does not speak it at all." [^212] Another critic writing in *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* was more complimentary, describing Olivier's performance as "not intellectually brilliant," but as combining 'intelligence with grace in an unusual degree." [^213] Olivier also played the role in [Elsinore Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsinore_Castle "Elsinore Castle") in 1937, directed by [Tyrone Guthrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrone_Guthrie "Tyrone Guthrie") and with [Vivien Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivien_Leigh "Vivien Leigh") as Ophelia.[^214] In 1963, Olivier directed [Peter O'Toole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_O'Toole "Peter O'Toole") in the inaugural performance of the [National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre"); critics found resonance between O'Toole's Hamlet and Jimmy Porter in [John Osborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Osborne "John Osborne") 's *[Look Back in Anger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Look_Back_in_Anger "Look Back in Anger")*.[^215] [^216]

Other significant British Hamlets have included [Alec Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alec_Guinness "Alec Guinness"),[^217] [Michael Redgrave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Redgrave "Michael Redgrave"),[^218] [David Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Warner_\(actor\) "David Warner (actor)"),[^219] [Albert Finney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Finney "Albert Finney"),[^220] and [Jonathan Pryce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Pryce "Jonathan Pryce").[^221] [Ian Charleson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Charleson "Ian Charleson") performed Hamlet in 1989 in [Richard Eyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Eyre "Richard Eyre") 's production at the [National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre"), replacing [Daniel Day-Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Day-Lewis "Daniel Day-Lewis"), who had withdrawn from the production. Seriously ill from [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS "HIV/AIDS") at the time, Charleson died eight weeks after his last performance. [Ian McKellen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_McKellen "Ian McKellen") said that Charleson played Hamlet so well it was as if he had rehearsed the role all his life, deeming it "the perfect Hamlet".[^222] [^223] The performance garnered other major accolades as well, some critics calling Charleson's Hamlet definitive.[^224] The 1995 [Almeida Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almeida_Theatre "Almeida Theatre") production with [Ralph Fiennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Fiennes "Ralph Fiennes") as Hamlet and [Francesca Annis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesca_Annis "Francesca Annis") as Gertrude, transferred to Broadway, where it garnered Fiennes a Best Actor [Tony Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Actor_in_a_Play "Tony Award for Best Actor in a Play").[^225]

[Richard Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Burton "Richard Burton") received his third Tony nomination when he played his second Hamlet, under Gielgud's direction, in a 1964 production that holds the record for the longest run of the play (137 performances) on Broadway. In 1968, [Joseph Papp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Papp "Joseph Papp") staged at [The Public Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Public_Theater "The Public Theater") what became known as "Naked" *Hamlet* because the text was stripped down; [Martin Sheen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Sheen "Martin Sheen") as Hamlet delivered the monologues either in Spanish or with a Spanish accent, as Hamlet's alter-ego, a Puerto Rican janitor named Ramon.[^226] [Stacy Keach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stacy_Keach "Stacy Keach") played Hamlet at the [Delacorte Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delacorte_Theater "Delacorte Theater") in the early 1970s, with [Colleen Dewhurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colleen_Dewhurst "Colleen Dewhurst") as Gertrude and [James Earl Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Earl_Jones "James Earl Jones") as Claudius. [Sam Waterston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Waterston "Sam Waterston"), the Laertes in that production, later played the title role himself at the Delacorte and then the [Vivian Beaumont Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Beaumont_Theater "Vivian Beaumont Theater"). Other North American productions starred [William Hurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hurt "William Hurt") [off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway "Off-Broadway"),[^227] [Christopher Walken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Walken "Christopher Walken") in Stratford, Connecticut,[^228] [Diane Venora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Venora "Diane Venora") at [The Public Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Public_Theater "The Public Theater"),[^229] and [Keanu Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keanu_Reeves "Keanu Reeves") at the [Royal Manitoba Theatre Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Manitoba_Theatre_Centre "Royal Manitoba Theatre Centre") in [Winnipeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winnipeg "Winnipeg").[^230]

### 21st century

*Hamlet* remains one of the most frequently staged of all plays. The [Internet Broadway Database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Broadway_Database "Internet Broadway Database") lists a total of 66 productions,[^231] and Theatricalia lists 324 in England.[^232] Actors performing the lead role since 2000 have included: [Mark Rylance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Rylance "Mark Rylance"),[^233] [Simon Russell Beale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Russell_Beale "Simon Russell Beale"),[^234] [Adrian Lester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Lester "Adrian Lester"),[^235] [Samuel West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_West "Samuel West"),[^236] [Christopher Eccleston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Eccleston "Christopher Eccleston"),[^237] [David Tennant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Tennant "David Tennant"),[^238] [^239] [Jude Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jude_Law "Jude Law"),[^239] [Rory Kinnear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rory_Kinnear "Rory Kinnear"),[^239] [Michael Sheen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Sheen "Michael Sheen"),[^239] [Maxine Peake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxine_Peake "Maxine Peake"),[^239] [Benedict Cumberbatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_Cumberbatch "Benedict Cumberbatch") [^239] and [Andrew Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Scott_\(actor\) "Andrew Scott (actor)").[^240]

The success of [Simon Russell Beale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Russell_Beale "Simon Russell Beale") 's *Hamlet* was ascribed, by fellow cast member Dennis Quilley, to Beale's insistence on ignoring the tradition that came before him, quoting him as saying "Let's forget Guilgud and Olivier and [John Neville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Neville_\(actor\) "John Neville (actor)"), and just see what the character means".[^235]

[Benedict Cumberbatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_Cumberbatch "Benedict Cumberbatch") 's 2015 run at the [Barbican Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbican_Centre "Barbican Centre") was called the "most in-demand theatre production of all time" and sold out seven hours after tickets went on sale, more than a year before the play opened.[^241] [^242]

### Film and TV performances

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bernhardt_Hamlet2.jpg)

Sarah Bernhardt as Hamlet, with Yorick 's skull (photographer: James Lafayette, c. 1885–1900 )

An early filmed sequence from *Hamlet* was [Sarah Bernhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Bernhardt "Sarah Bernhardt") 's 1900 five-minute film of the fencing scene [^243]. The film was an early attempt at combining [sound and film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_film "Sound film"); music and words were recorded on phonograph records, to be played along with the film.[^244] Silent versions were released in 1907, 1908, 1910, 1913, 1917, and 1920.[^245] In the 1921 film *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(1921_film\) "Hamlet (1921 film)")*, Danish actress [Asta Nielsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asta_Nielsen "Asta Nielsen") played the role of Hamlet as a woman who spends her life disguised as a man.[^244]

[Laurence Olivier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Olivier "Laurence Olivier") 's 1948 moody black-and-white *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(1948_film\) "Hamlet (1948 film)")* won [Best Picture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Picture "Academy Award for Best Picture") and [Best Actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Actor "Academy Award for Best Actor") [Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Awards "Academy Awards") and as of 2026 is the only Shakespeare film to have done so. His interpretation stressed the Oedipal overtones of the play and cast 28-year-old [Eileen Herlie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eileen_Herlie "Eileen Herlie") as Hamlet's mother opposite himself at 41 as Hamlet.[^246] In 1953, actor [Jack Manning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Manning_\(actor\) "Jack Manning (actor)") performed the play in 15-minute segments over two weeks in the [DuMont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuMont_Television_Network "DuMont Television Network") series *[Monodrama Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodrama_Theater "Monodrama Theater")*. *New York Times* TV critic Jack Gould praised Manning's performance.[^247] The 1964 Soviet film *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(1964_film\) "Hamlet (1964 film)")* (Russian: Гамлет) is based on a translation by [Boris Pasternak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Pasternak "Boris Pasternak") and directed by [Grigori Kozintsev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Kozintsev "Grigori Kozintsev"), with a score by [Dmitri Shostakovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Shostakovich "Dmitri Shostakovich").[^248] [Innokenty Smoktunovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innokenty_Smoktunovsky "Innokenty Smoktunovsky") was cast in the title role.[^249]

John Gielgud directed [Richard Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Burton "Richard Burton") in a [Broadway production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Burton's_Hamlet "Richard Burton's Hamlet") in 1964–65, the longest-running *Hamlet* in the U.S. to date. A live film of the production was produced using "Electronovision", a method of recording a live performance with multiple video cameras and converting the image to film. Eileen Herlie repeated her role from Olivier's film version as the Queen,[^250] and the voice of Gielgud was heard as the ghost.[^251] The Gielgud/Burton production was also recorded and released in theatres.[^250]

[Franco Zeffirelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Zeffirelli "Franco Zeffirelli"), whose Shakespeare films have been described as "sensual rather than cerebral",[^252] cast [Mel Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mel_Gibson "Mel Gibson") in the title role of his [1990 movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(1990_film\) "Hamlet (1990 film)"), with [Glenn Close](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Close "Glenn Close") as Gertrude.[^253] The 1994 animated musical film [The Lion King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lion_King "The Lion King") has elements inspired by Hamlet's story.[^254]

[Kenneth Branagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Branagh "Kenneth Branagh") adapted, directed, and starred in a 1996 version that contained material from both the First Folio and the Second Quarto. Branagh's [*Hamlet*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(1996_film\) "Hamlet (1996 film)") was the first unabridged theatrical film adaptation of the play and has a runtime of 242 minutes.[^255] [^256] Branagh set the film with late 19th-century costuming and furnishings, a production in many ways reminiscent of a Russian novel of the time,[^257] and [Blenheim Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blenheim_Palace "Blenheim Palace"), built in the early 18th century, became Elsinore Castle in the external scenes. The film is structured as an [epic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_film "Epic film") and makes frequent use of [flashbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashback_\(narrative\) "Flashback (narrative)") to highlight elements not made explicit in the play: Hamlet's sexual relationship with [Kate Winslet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Winslet "Kate Winslet") 's Ophelia, for example, or his childhood affection for Yorick (played by [Ken Dodd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Dodd "Ken Dodd")).[^258]

In 2000, [Michael Almereyda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Almereyda "Michael Almereyda") 's *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(2000_film\) "Hamlet (2000 film)")* set the story in contemporary [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan "Manhattan"), with [Ethan Hawke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethan_Hawke "Ethan Hawke") playing Hamlet as a film student. Claudius (played by [Kyle MacLachlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_MacLachlan "Kyle MacLachlan")) became the CEO of "Denmark Corporation", having taken over the company by killing his brother.[^259] The 2014 [Bollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bollywood "Bollywood") film *[Haider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haider_\(film\) "Haider (film)")* is an adaptation set in modern [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir "Kashmir").[^260] The [Tribeca Film Festival 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribeca_Festival "Tribeca Festival"), organized at the Public Theater, hosted the world premiere of a completely audio production of the play by Make-Believe Association with Daniel Kyri in the title role.[^261] It continues as a six-part podcast.[^262] [^263]

## Derivative works

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ralph_Cleaver_-_1904_amateur_performance_of_W.S._Gilbert's_Rosencrantz_and_Guildenstern_at_the_Garrick_Theatre,_London_-_Image_1.jpg)

Scenes from a 1904 benefit performance of W. S. Gilbert 's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, with Gilbert as Claudius

This section is limited to derivative works written for the stage.

Tom Stoppard's 1966 play *[Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosencrantz_and_Guildenstern_Are_Dead "Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead")* retells many of the events of the story from the point of view of the characters Rosencrantz and Guildenstern and gives them a backstory of their own. Several times since 1995, the [American Shakespeare Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Shakespeare_Center "American Shakespeare Center") has mounted repertories that included both *Hamlet* and *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern*, with the same actors performing the same roles in each.[^264]

[W. S. Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._S._Gilbert "W. S. Gilbert") wrote a short comic play titled *[Rosencrantz and Guildenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosencrantz_and_Guildenstern_\(play\) "Rosencrantz and Guildenstern (play)")*, in which Hamlet's play is presented as a tragedy written by Claudius in his youth of which he is greatly embarrassed. Through the chaos triggered by Hamlet's staging of it, Guildenstern helps Rosencrantz vie with Hamlet to make Ophelia his bride.[^265]

[Lee Blessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Blessing "Lee Blessing") 's *[Fortinbras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortinbras_\(play\) "Fortinbras (play)")* is a comical sequel to *Hamlet* in which all the deceased characters come back as ghosts. *The New York Times* said it is "scarcely more than an extended comedy sketch, lacking the portent and linguistic complexity of Tom Stoppard's *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*. *Fortinbras* operates on a far less ambitious plane, but it is a ripping yarn and offers Keith Reddin a role in which he can commit comic mayhem".[^266]

[Caridad Svich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caridad_Svich "Caridad Svich") 's *12 Ophelias (a play with broken songs)* includes elements of the story of *Hamlet* but focuses on Ophelia. In Svich's play, Ophelia is resurrected and rises from a pool of water, after her death in *Hamlet*. The play is a series of scenes and songs, and was first staged at a public swimming pool in Brooklyn.[^267]

[David Davalos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Davalos "David Davalos") 's *Wittenberg* is a "tragical-comical-historical" prequel to *Hamlet* that depicts the Danish prince as a student at Wittenberg University (now known as the [University of Halle-Wittenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Halle-Wittenberg "University of Halle-Wittenberg")), where he is torn between the conflicting teachings of his mentors [John Faustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faust "Faust") and [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther "Martin Luther"). *The New York Times* reviewed the play, saying, "Mr. Davalos has molded a daft campus comedy out of this unlikely convergence",[^268] and *[Nytheatre.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nytheatre.com "Nytheatre.com")* 's review said the playwright "has imagined a fascinating alternate reality, and quite possibly, given the fictional Hamlet a back story that will inform the role for the future." [^269]

*Mad Boy Chronicle* by Canadian playwright Michael O'Brien is a dark comedy loosely based on *Hamlet*, set in [Viking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age "Viking Age") Denmark in 999 AD.[^270]

In 2025 [Radiohead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiohead "Radiohead") 's [Thom Yorke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom_Yorke "Thom Yorke") collaborated with directors Steven Hoggett and Christine Jones at the [Royal Shakespeare Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Shakespeare_Company "Royal Shakespeare Company") to make a work fusing *Hamlet* with Radiohead's album *[Hail to the Thief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hail_to_the_Thief "Hail to the Thief")*. The work featured [Samuel Blenkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Blenkin "Samuel Blenkin") as Hamlet.[^271]
