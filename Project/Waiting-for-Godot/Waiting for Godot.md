***Waiting for Godot*** [^1] is a play by Irish playwright and author [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett"). It was written (1948–1949), first published (1952), and first performed (1953) in French as ****En attendant Godot****.[^3] The play is Beckett's own English-language adaptation of the original. Subtitled as a " [tragicomedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy "Tragicomedy") in two acts",[^3] it is his best-known literary work and regarded by critics as "one of the most enigmatic plays of modern literature".[^4] [^5] In a 1998 public poll conducted by London's [Royal National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre"), *Waiting for Godot* was voted as "the most significant English-language play of the 20th century".[^6] [^7] [^8]

In *Waiting for Godot*, the two main characters, [Vladimir (Didi)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Vladimir (Waiting for Godot)") and [Estragon (Gogo)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estragon "Estragon"), spend their days waiting for someone named Godot, who they believe will provide them with salvation. They pass the time with conversations, physical routines, and philosophical musings, but Godot never arrives.[^9] They encounter three other characters: Pozzo; his servant, Lucky; and a young boy. As the play unfolds, the repetition of actions and dialogue suggests the cyclical nature of their lives, and though Godot is promised for "tomorrow", he never appears in the play, leaving the characters in a state of existential uncertainty.

The original French text was composed between 9 October 1948 and 29 January 1949.[^10] The [premiere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiere "Premiere"), directed by [Roger Blin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blin "Roger Blin"), was at the [Théâtre de Babylone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9%C3%A2tre_de_Babylone?action=edit&redlink=1 "Théâtre de Babylone (page does not exist)"), Paris, in January 1953. The English-language version of the play premiered in London in 1955. Though there is only one scene throughout both acts, the play is known for its numerous themes, religious, philosophical, classical, social, psychoanalytical, and biographical. Beckett said [Caspar David Friedrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspar_David_Friedrich "Caspar David Friedrich") 's 1819 painting *[Two Men Contemplating the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Men_Contemplating_the_Moon "Two Men Contemplating the Moon")* was a major inspiration for the play. Dramatist [Martin Esslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Esslin "Martin Esslin") said *Waiting for Godot* was part of a broader literary movement known as the [Theatre of the Absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Absurd "Theatre of the Absurd"), which was first proposed by [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus").

The play has received widespread acclaim. Due to its popularity, significance, and cultural importance to modern literature, *Waiting for Godot* has often been adapted for stage, operas, musicals, television, and theatrical performances across most of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe") and the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas"). As one of the foundational works of modern absurdist theatre,[^11] the play remains widely studied in academic and literary circles.

## Plot

### Act I

The play opens with two bedraggled acquaintances, Vladimir and Estragon, meeting by a leafless tree. Estragon notifies Vladimir of his most recent troubles: he spent the previous night lying in a ditch and received a beating from a number of anonymous assailants. The duo discuss various matters at length, none of any apparent significance, and it is eventually revealed that they are awaiting a man named Godot. They are not certain if they have ever met Godot, nor if he will even arrive.

Subsequently, an imperious traveller named [Pozzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozzo_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Pozzo (Waiting for Godot)"), along with his silent slave Lucky, arrives and pauses to converse with Vladimir and Estragon. Lucky is bound by a rope held by Pozzo, who forces Lucky to carry his heavy bags and physically punishes him if he deems Lucky's movements too lethargic. Pozzo says he is on the way to the market, at which he intends to sell Lucky for profit. Following Pozzo's command "Think!", the otherwise mute Lucky performs a sudden dance and monologue: a torrent of academic-sounding nonsensical phrases, albeit carefully constructed to deliver "poetic rationality". Lucky's speech seems to cryptically reference the play's absurdist themes.[^12] Pozzo and Lucky depart, leaving the bewildered Estragon and Vladimir to continue their wait for the absent Godot.

Eventually, a boy arrives and tells Vladimir and Estragon that he is a messenger from Godot, and that Godot will not be arriving tonight, but surely tomorrow. Vladimir asks for descriptions of Godot, receiving only extremely brief or vague answers from the boy, who soon exits. Vladimir and Estragon then announce that they will also leave, but they remain onstage without moving.

### Act II

Vladimir and Estragon are again waiting near the tree, which has grown several leaves since Act 1. They are still awaiting Godot. Lucky and Pozzo eventually reappear, but not as they were previously. Pozzo has become blind and Lucky is now fully mute. Pozzo cannot recall having met Vladimir and Estragon, who themselves cannot agree on when they last saw the travellers. Lucky and Pozzo exit shortly after their spirited encounter, leaving Vladimir and Estragon to go on waiting.

Soon after, the boy reappears to again report that Godot will not be coming tonight, but surely tomorrow. The boy says he has not met Vladimir and Estragon before and is not the same boy who spoke to Vladimir yesterday, which causes Vladimir to burst into a rage at the child, demanding that the boy remember him the next day so as to avoid repeating this encounter once more. After the boy exits, Vladimir and Estragon consider suicide, but they do not have a rope with which to hang themselves. They decide to leave and return the day after with a rope, but again they merely remain motionless as the scene fades to black.

## Characters

Beckett refrained from elaborating on the characters beyond what he had written in the play. He once recalled that when Sir [Ralph Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Richardson "Ralph Richardson") "wanted the low-down on Pozzo, his home address and curriculum vitae, and seemed to make the forthcoming of this and similar information the condition of his condescending to illustrate the part of Vladimir... I told him that all I knew about Pozzo was in the text, that if I had known more I would have put it in the text, and that was true also of the other characters." [^13]

### Vladimir and Estragon

**Appearance**

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Waiting_for_Godot_in_Doon_School.jpg)

Vladimir and Estragon ( The Doon School, India, 2010)

When Beckett started writing he did not have a visual image of Vladimir and Estragon. They are never referred to as tramps in the text, though they are often performed in tramps' costumes. [Roger Blin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blin "Roger Blin") said: "Beckett heard their voices, but he couldn't describe his characters to me. \[He said\]: 'The only thing I'm sure of is that they're wearing bowlers.'" [^14] "The bowler hat was of course *de rigueur* for men in many social contexts when Beckett was growing up in [Foxrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxrock "Foxrock"), and \[his father\] William Beckett commonly wore one." [^15] The play does indicate that the clothes worn at least by Estragon are shabby. When told by Vladimir that he should have been a poet, Estragon says he was, gestures to his rags, and asks if it is not obvious.

There are no physical descriptions of either character, but the text indicates that Vladimir is the heavier of the pair, as revealed in the contemplation-of-suicide scene. The bowlers and other broadly comic aspects of their personae have reminded modern audiences of [Laurel and Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurel_and_Hardy "Laurel and Hardy"), who occasionally played tramps in their films. In *The Comic Mind: Comedy and the Movies*. [Gerald Mast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Mast "Gerald Mast") wrote, "The hat-passing game in *Waiting for Godot* and Lucky's inability to think without his hat on are two obvious Beckett derivations from Laurel and Hardy – a substitution of form for essence, covering for reality".[^16] Their "blather", which includes [Hiberno-English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English "Hiberno-English") idioms, indicates that they are [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_people "Irish people").[^17]

**Physicality and beliefs** Vladimir stands through most of the play, while Estragon sits numerous times and even dozes off. "Estragon is inert and Vladimir restless." [^18] Vladimir looks at the sky and muses on religious or philosophical matters. Estragon "belongs to the stone",[^19] preoccupied with mundane things such as what he can get to eat and how to ease his physical aches and pains; he is direct, intuitive. The monotonous, ritualistic means by which Estragon continuously sits upon the stone may be likened to [Winnie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Days_\(play\)#Winnie "Happy Days (play)") 's constant nail filing in Beckett's play *[Happy Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Days_\(play\) "Happy Days (play)")*; both actions can be seen as representing the slow, deliberate erosion of the characters' lives. Estragon finds it hard to remember but can recall certain things when prompted, *e.g.*, when Vladimir asks: "Do you remember the [Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel#Canonical_gospels "Gospel")?" [^20] Estragon tells Vladimir about the coloured maps of the [Holy Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land "Holy Land") and that he planned to honeymoon by the [Dead Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea "Dead Sea"); it is his short-term memory that is poorest and suggests that he may in fact have dementia.[^21] [Al Alvarez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Alvarez "Al Alvarez") writes: "But perhaps Estragon's forgetfulness is the cement binding their relationship together. He continually forgets, Vladimir continually reminds him; between them they pass the time." [^22] Estragon's forgetfulness also affords Beckett a certain narrative utility, allowing his mundane, empty conversations with Vladimir to continue seamlessly. They have been together for fifty years but when asked by Pozzo they do not reveal their actual ages. Vladimir's life is not without its discomforts too but he is the more resilient of the pair. "Vladimir's pain is primarily mental anguish, which would thus account for his voluntary exchange of his hat for Lucky's, thus signifying Vladimir's symbolic desire for another person's thoughts." [^23] To some, these characterizations represent the act of thinking or mental state (Vladimir) and physical things or the body (Estragon).[^24] This is depicted by Vladimir's continuous attention to his hat and Estragon's to his boots. The two characters are temperamentally opposite, but are both essential: Vladimir's metaphysical musings are balanced by Estragon's physical demands.[^25]

**Interpretations** The above characterizations, particularly those about their existential situation, are also demonstrated in one of the play's recurring themes, which is sleep.[^26] Twice, Estragon falls asleep and has nightmares, about which he wants to tell Vladimir when he wakes. The latter refuses, since he cannot tolerate the sense of entrapment the dreamer experiences. This idea of entrapment supports the view that the play's setting may be understood more clearly as a dreamlike landscape, or, a form of [Purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory "Purgatory"), from which neither man can escape. One interpretation noted the link between the two characters' experiences and the way they represent them: the impotence in Estragon's nightmare and Vladimir's predicament of waiting as his companion sleeps.[^26] It is also said that sleep and impatience allow the spectators to distinguish the two characters: that sleep expresses Estragon's focus on his sensations while Vladimir's restlessness shows his focus on his thoughts.[^27] This is indicative of what some call a pattern of duality in the play.[^28] In the case of the protagonists, the duality involves the body and the mind, making the characters complementary.[^27]

**Nicknames**

Throughout the play the couple refer to each other by the pet names "Didi" and "Gogo", although the boy addresses Vladimir as "Mister Albert". Beckett originally intended to call Estragon "Lévy" but when Pozzo questions him he gives his name as "Magrégor, André" [^29] and also responds to " *Catulle* " in French or " [Catullus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catullus "Catullus") " in the first Faber edition. This became "Adam" in the American edition. Beckett's only explanation was that he was "fed up with Catullus".[^30]

**Characterisation reception**

[Vivian Mercier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Mercier "Vivian Mercier") described *Waiting for Godot* as a play which "has achieved a theoretical impossibility – a play in which nothing happens, that yet keeps audiences glued to their seats. What's more, since the second act is a subtly different reprise of the first, he has written a play in which nothing happens, twice." [^31] Mercier once questioned Beckett on the language used by the pair: "It seemed to me... he made Didi and Gogo sound as if they had earned PhDs. 'How do you know they hadn't?' was his reply.[^32] They clearly have known better times, such as a visit to the [Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower "Eiffel Tower") and grape-harvesting by the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne "Rhône"); this is about all either has to say about their pasts, save for Estragon's claim to have been a poet, an explanation Estragon provides to Vladimir for his destitution. In the first stage production, which Beckett oversaw, both are "more shabby-genteel than ragged... Vladimir at least is capable of being scandalised... on a matter of [etiquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiquette "Etiquette") when Estragon [begs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Begging "Begging") for chicken bones or money." [^33]

### Pozzo and Lucky

**Characterisation**

Pozzo and Lucky have been together for 60 years.[^34] Pozzo controls Lucky by means of an extremely long rope, which he jerks and tugs if Lucky is the least bit slow.

Lucky appears to be the subservient member of their relationship, at least initially, carrying out every task that Pozzo bids him to do without question, portraying a form of "dog-like devotion" to his *master.*[^35] He struggles with a heavy suitcase, falling on a number of occasions, only to be helped and held up by Estragon and Vladimir.

It has been contended that " [Pozzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozzo_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Pozzo (Waiting for Godot)") and Lucky are simply Didi and Gogo writ large", unbalanced as their relationship is.[^36] However, Pozzo's dominance is superficial; "upon closer inspection, it becomes evident that Lucky always possessed more influence in the relationship, for he danced, and more importantly, thought – not as a service, but in order to fill a vacant need of Pozzo: he committed all of these acts *for* Pozzo. As such, since the first appearance of the duo, the true [slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery "Slavery") had always been Pozzo." [^23] Pozzo credits Lucky with having given him all the culture, refinement, and ability to reason that he possesses. His [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric") has been learned by rote. Pozzo's "party piece" on the sky is a clear example: as his memory crumbles, he finds himself unable to continue under his own steam.

**Emphasis of contrasts**

Little is learned about Pozzo besides the fact that he is on his way to the fair to sell his slave, Lucky. From Beckett's own life experiences in Ireland and wartime France, commentators such as [Hugh Kenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Kenner "Hugh Kenner") have identified Pozzo as representing German behaviour in occupied France, or alternatively as a bullying and conceited [Protestant Ascendancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy "Protestant Ascendancy") landlord.[^37] When translating his original French dialogue into English, Beckett took pains to introduce Irish idiom (specifically, Dubliners' idiom): Pozzo's [pipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_pipe "Tobacco pipe") is made by [Kapp and Peterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterson_Pipes "Peterson Pipes"), Dublin's best-known tobacconists (which he refers to as a " [briar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erica_arborea#Uses "Erica arborea") " but which Estragon calls by the dialect word *dudeen*). Not only is his [Hiberno-English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English "Hiberno-English") text more colourful than the French original, but it emphasizes the differences in the characters' social standing.[^38] [^39] Pozzo confesses to a poor memory but it is more a result of an abiding self-absorption. "Pozzo is a character who has to overcompensate. That's why he overdoes things... and his overcompensation has to do with a deep insecurity in him. These were things Beckett said, psychological terms he used." [^40] Beckett's advice to the American director [Alan Schneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Schneider "Alan Schneider") was: "\[Pozzo\] is a [hypomaniac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypomania "Hypomania") and the only way to play him is to play him mad." [^18]

"In his \[English\] translation... Beckett struggled to retain the French atmosphere as much as possible, so that he delegated all the English names and places to Lucky, whose own name, he thought, suggested such a correlation".[^41]

**Characterisation reception**

Lucky speaks only once in the play and it is in response to Pozzo's order to "think" for Estragon and Vladimir. The ostensibly abstract philosophical meanderings supplied to the audience by Lucky during his speech have been described as "a flood of completely meaningless gibberish" by [Martin Esslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Esslin "Martin Esslin") in his essay, ["The Theatre of the Absurd"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Absurd#Etymology "Theatre of the Absurd").[^42] Esslin suggests that this seemingly involuntary, philosophical spouting is an example of the actor's working "against the dialogue rather than with it",[^42] providing grounds for Esslin's claims that the "fervor of delivery" in the play must "stand in a dialectical contrast to the pointlessness of the meaning of the lines".[^42]

[Jean Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Martin "Jean Martin"), who originated the role of [Lucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucky_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Lucky (Waiting for Godot)") in Paris in 1953, spoke to a doctor named Marthe Gautier, who was working at the [Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piti%C3%A9-Salp%C3%AAtri%C3%A8re_Hospital "Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital"). Martin asked if she knew of a [physiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology "Physiology") reason that would explain Lucky's voice as it was written in the text. Gautier suggested [Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease "Parkinson's disease"), which, she said, "begins with a trembling, which gets more and more noticeable, until later the patient can no longer speak without the voice shaking". Martin began incorporating this idea into his rehearsals.[^43] Beckett and the director may not have been completely convinced, but they expressed no objections.[^44] When Martin mentioned to the playwright that he was "playing Lucky as if he were suffering from Parkinson's", Beckett responded by saying "Yes, of course", and mentioning that his own mother had Parkinson's.[^45]

When Beckett was asked why Lucky was so named, he replied, "I suppose he is lucky to have no more expectations".[^46]

### The Boy

![Estragon and the boy in dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Waiting_for_Godot_University_of_Chicago_II.jpg)

Estragon and the boy ( University of Chicago, 2020)

The cast list specifies only one boy.

The boy in Act I, a local lad, assures Vladimir that this is the first time he has seen him. He says he was not there the previous day. He confirms he works for Mr. Godot as a [goatherd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goatherd "Goatherd"). His brother, whom Godot beats, is a [shepherd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd "Shepherd"). Godot feeds both of them and allows them to sleep in his hayloft.

The boy in Act II also assures Vladimir that it was not he who called upon them the day before. He insists that this too is his first visit. When Vladimir asks what Godot does, the boy tells him, "He does nothing, sir." [^47] We also learn he has a white beard – possibly, the boy is not certain. This boy also has a brother who it seems is sick but there is no clear evidence to suggest that his brother is the boy who came in Act I or the one who came the day before that.

Whether the boy from Act I is the same boy from Act II or not, both boys are polite yet timid. In the first act, the boy, despite claiming to have arrived while Pozzo and Lucky are still about, does not announce himself until after Pozzo and Lucky leave, saying to Vladimir and Estragon that he waited for the other two to leave out of fear of the two men and of Pozzo's whip; the boy does not claim to arrive early enough in Act II to see either Lucky or Pozzo. In both acts, the boy seems hesitant to speak very much, saying mostly "Yes Sir" or "No Sir", and winds up exiting by running away.

### Godot

The identity of Godot has been the subject of much debate. "When Colin Duckworth asked Beckett point-blank whether Pozzo was Godot, the author replied: 'No. It is just implied in the text, but it's not true.'" [^48]

**Possible inspirations**

[Deirdre Bair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deirdre_Bair "Deirdre Bair") says that though "Beckett will never discuss the implications of the title", she suggests two stories that both may have at least partially inspired it. The first is that because feet are a recurring theme in the play, Beckett has said the title was suggested to him by the slang French term for boot: " *[godillot](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/godillot "wikt:godillot")*, *[godasse](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/godasse "wikt:godasse")* ". The second story, according to Bair, is that Beckett once encountered a group of spectators at the French *Tour de France* bicycle race, who told him "Nous attendons Godot" – they were waiting for a competitor whose name was Godot.[^49]

"Beckett said to [Peter Woodthorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Woodthorpe "Peter Woodthorpe") that he regretted calling the absent character 'Godot', because of all the theories involving God to which this had given rise." [^50] "I also told \[Ralph\] Richardson that if by Godot I had meant God I would \[have\] said God, and not Godot. This seemed to disappoint him greatly." [^51] That said, Beckett did once concede, "It would be fatuous of me to pretend that I am not aware of the meanings attached to the word 'Godot', and the opinion of many that it means 'God'. But you must remember – I wrote the play in French, and if I did have that meaning in my mind, it was somewhere in my unconscious and I was not overtly aware of it." [^52] (Note: the French word for 'God' is 'Dieu'.) However, "Beckett has often stressed the strong unconscious impulses that partly control his writing; he has even spoken of being 'in a [trance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance "Trance") ' when he writes." [^53] While Beckett stated he originally had no knowledge of [Balzac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honor%C3%A9_de_Balzac "Honoré de Balzac") 's play *Mercadet ou le faiseur*, whose character Godeau has an identical-sounding name and is involved in a similar situation, it has been suggested he may have been instead influenced by *[The Lovable Cheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lovable_Cheat "The Lovable Cheat")*,[^54] a minor adaptation of *Mercadet* featuring [Buster Keaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buster_Keaton "Buster Keaton"), whose works Beckett had admired,[^55] and whom he later sought out for *[Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_\(film\) "Film (film)")*.

Unlike elsewhere in Beckett's work, no bicycle appears in this play, but [Hugh Kenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Kenner "Hugh Kenner") in his essay "The Cartesian Centaur" [^56] reports that Beckett once, when asked about the meaning of Godot, mentioned "a veteran racing cyclist, bald, a 'stayer', recurrent placeman in town-to-town and national championships, Christian name elusive, surname Godeau, pronounced, of course, no differently from Godot." *Waiting for Godot* is clearly not about track cycling, but it is said that Beckett himself did wait for French cyclist [Roger Godeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Godeau?action=edit&redlink=1 "Roger Godeau (page does not exist)") (1920–2000; a professional cyclist from 1943 to 1961), outside the velodrome in [Roubaix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roubaix "Roubaix").[^57] [^58]

Of the two boys who work for Godot only one appears safe from beatings, "Beckett said, only half-jokingly, that one of Estragon's feet was saved".[^59]

**Pronunciation**

The name "Godot" is pronounced in Britain and Ireland with the emphasis on the first syllable, [/ ˈ ɡ ɒ d oʊ /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*GOD-oh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key");[^9] in North America it is usually pronounced with an emphasis on the second syllable, [/ ɡ ə ˈ d oʊ /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*gə-DOH*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"). Beckett himself said the emphasis should be on the first syllable, and that the North American pronunciation is a mistake.[^60] [Georges Borchardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Borchardt "Georges Borchardt"), Beckett's literary agent, and who represents Beckett's literary estate, has always pronounced "Godot" in the French manner, with equal emphasis on both syllables. Borchardt checked with Beckett's nephew, Edward, who told him his uncle pronounced it that way as well.[^9] The 1956 [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre "Broadway theatre") production split the difference by having Vladimir pronounce "Godot" with equal stress on both syllables (goh-doh) and Estragon pronounce it with the accent on the second syllable (g'doh).[^61] [^62]

## Setting

There is only one scene throughout both acts. Two men are waiting on a country road by a tree. The men are of unspecified origin, though it is clear that they are not English by nationality since they refer to currency as [francs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franc "Franc"), and tell derisive jokes about the English – and in English-language productions the pair are traditionally played with [Irish accents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English "Hiberno-English"). The script calls for Estragon to sit on a low mound but in practice – as in Beckett's own 1975 German production – this is usually a stone. In the first act the tree is bare. In the second, a few leaves have appeared despite the script specifying that it is the next day. The minimal description calls to mind "the idea of the *lieu vague*, a location which should not be particularised".[^63]

Other clues about the location can be found in the dialogue. In Act I, Vladimir turns toward the auditorium and describes it as a bog. In Act II, Vladimir again motions to the auditorium and notes that there is "Not a soul in sight." When Estragon rushes toward the back of the stage in Act II, Vladimir scolds him, saying that "There's no way out there." Also in Act II, Vladimir comments that their surroundings look nothing like the Macon country, and Estragon states that he's lived his whole life "Here! In the Cackon country!"

[Alan Schneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Schneider "Alan Schneider") once suggested putting the play on in the round – Pozzo has been described as a [ringmaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringmaster_\(circus\) "Ringmaster (circus)") [^64] – but Beckett dissuaded him: "I don't in my ignorance agree with the round and feel *Godot* needs a very closed box." He even contemplated at one point having a "faint shadow of bars on stage floor" but, in the end, decided against this level of what he called "explicitation".[^65] In Beckett's 1975 [Schiller Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiller_Theater "Schiller Theater") production in Berlin, there are times when Didi and Gogo appear to bounce off something "like birds trapped in the strands of \[an invisible\] net", in James Knowlson's description.[^66]

## Interpretations

"Because the play is so stripped down, so elemental, it invites all kinds of social and political and religious interpretation", wrote Normand Berlin in a tribute to the play in autumn 1999, "with Beckett himself placed in different schools of thought, different movements and 'isms'. The attempts to pin him down have not been successful, but the desire to do so is natural when we encounter a writer whose minimalist art reaches for bedrock reality. 'Less' forces us to look for 'more', and the need to talk about *Godot* and about Beckett has resulted in a steady outpouring of books and articles." [^6] [^67]

Throughout *Waiting for Godot*, the audience may encounter [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion"), philosophical, [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), [psychoanalytical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalysis "Psychoanalysis") and biographical – especially [wartime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance") – references. There are [ritualistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual "Ritual") aspects and elements taken directly from [vaudeville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaudeville "Vaudeville"),[^68] and there is a danger in making more of these than what they are: that is, merely structural conveniences, avatars into which the writer places his fictional characters. The play "exploits several archetypal forms and situations, all of which lend themselves to both comedy and pathos." [^69] Beckett makes this point emphatically in the opening notes to *[Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_\(film\) "Film (film)")*: "No truth value attaches to the above, regarded as of merely structural and dramatic convenience." [^70] He told critic [Lawrence E. Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_E._Harvey?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lawrence E. Harvey (page does not exist)") that his "work does not depend on experience – \[it is\] not a record of experience. Of course you use it." [^71]

Beckett tired quickly of "the endless misunderstanding". As far back as 1955, he remarked, "Why people have to complicate a thing so simple I can't make out." [^72] He was not forthcoming with anything more than cryptic clues: " [Peter Woodthorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Woodthorpe "Peter Woodthorpe") \[who played Estragon\] remembered asking him one day in a taxi what the play was really about: 'It's all [symbiosis](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/symbiosis "wikt:symbiosis"), Peter; it's symbiosis,' answered Beckett." [^73]

Beckett directed the play for the Schiller-Theater in Berlin in 1975. Although he had overseen many productions, this was the first time that he had taken complete control. [Walter Asmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Asmus "Walter Asmus") was his conscientious young assistant director. The production was not naturalistic. Beckett explained:

> It is a game, everything is a game. When all four of them are lying on the ground, that cannot be handled naturalistically. That has got to be done artificially, balletically. Otherwise everything becomes an imitation, an imitation of reality \[...\] It should become clear and transparent, not dry. It is a game in order to survive.[^74]

Over the years, Beckett realised that much of *Godot* 's success was due to its openness to various readings and that this was not necessarily bad. He sanctioned "one of the most famous mixed-race productions of *Godot*, performed at the Baxter Theatre in the [University of Cape Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cape_Town "University of Cape Town"), directed by [Donald Howarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Howarth "Donald Howarth"), with \[...\] two black actors, [John Kani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kani "John Kani") and [Winston Ntshona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Ntshona "Winston Ntshona"), playing Didi and Gogo; Pozzo, dressed in checked shirt and gumboots reminiscent of an Afrikaner landlord, and Lucky ('a shanty town piece of white trash' [^75]) were played by two white actors, [Bill Flynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Flynn "Bill Flynn") and Peter Piccolo \[...\]. The Baxter production has often been portrayed as if it were an explicitly political production, when in fact it received very little emphasis. What such a reaction showed, however, was that, although the play can in no way be taken as a political allegory, there are elements that are relevant to any local situation in which one man is being exploited or oppressed by another." [^76]

### Political

"It was seen as an allegory of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") " [^77] or of [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance") to the Germans. Graham Hassell writes, "the intrusion of Pozzo and Lucky \[...\] seems like nothing more than a metaphor for Ireland's view of mainland Britain, where society has ever been blighted by a greedy ruling élite keeping the working classes passive and ignorant by whatever means." [^78]

The play was written shortly after the end of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), during which Beckett and his partner were forced to flee occupied Paris to avoid arrest, owing to [their affiliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett#World_War_II_and_French_Resistance "Samuel Beckett") with the French Resistance. After the war, Beckett volunteered for the [Red Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Committee_of_the_Red_Cross "International Committee of the Red Cross") in [Saint-Lô](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-L%C3%B4 "Saint-Lô"), which had been almost completely destroyed during the [D-Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-Day_\(military_term\) "D-Day (military term)") fighting. These experiences likely had a severe effect on both Beckett's politics and his views on the prevailing policies of the period.[^79] Some academics have theorized that *Godot* is set during World War II, with Estragon and Vladimir two Jews waiting for Godot to smuggle them out of occupied France.[^80]

### Psychological

#### Freudian

"Bernard Dukore develops a triadic theory in *Didi, Gogo and the absent Godot*, based on [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud") 's trinitarian description of the [psyche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psyche_\(psychology\) "Psyche (psychology)") in *[The Ego and the Id](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ego_and_the_Id "The Ego and the Id")* (1923) and the usage of [onomastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onomastics "Onomastics") techniques. Dukore defines the characters by what they lack: the rational Go-go embodies the incomplete ego, the missing [pleasure principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleasure_principle_\(psychology\) "Pleasure principle (psychology)"): (e)go-(e)go. Di-di (id-id) – who is more instinctual and irrational – is seen as the backward id or subversion of the rational principle. Godot fulfills the function of the superego or moral standards. Pozzo and Lucky are just re-iterations of the main protagonists. Dukore finally sees Beckett's play as a metaphor for the futility of man's existence when salvation is expected from an external entity, and the self is denied introspection." [^81]

#### Jungian

"The four archetypal personalities or the four aspects of the soul are grouped in two pairs: the [ego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_psychology#Complexes "Analytical psychology") and the [shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_psychology#Shadow "Analytical psychology"), the persona and the soul's image ([animus or anima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animus_or_anima?action=edit&redlink=1 "Animus or anima (page does not exist)")). The shadow is the container of all our despised emotions repressed by the ego. Lucky, the shadow, serves as the polar opposite of the egocentric Pozzo, prototype of prosperous mediocrity, who incessantly controls and persecutes his subordinate, thus symbolising the oppression of the unconscious shadow by the despotic ego. Lucky's monologue in Act I appears as a manifestation of a stream of repressed unconsciousness, as he is allowed to 'think' for his master. Estragon's name has another connotation, besides that of the aromatic herb, tarragon: "estragon" sounds similar to estrogen, the female hormone (Carter, 130). This prompts us to identify him with the [anima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_\(Jung\) "Anima (Jung)"), the feminine image of Vladimir's soul. It explains Estragon's propensity for poetry, his sensitivity and dreams, his irrational moods. Vladimir appears as the complementary masculine principle, or perhaps the rational persona of the contemplative type." [^82]

### Philosophical

#### Existential

Broadly speaking, [existentialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism") hold that everyone must come to terms with certain fundamental questions if they are to take their subjective existences seriously and with intrinsic value—questions such as life, death, the meaning of human existence and the place of God in that existence. By and large, the theories of existentialism assert that conscious reality is very complex and without an "objective" or universally known value: the individual must create value by affirming it and living it, not by simply talking about it or philosophising it in the mind. The play may be seen to touch on all these issues.

[Martin Esslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Esslin "Martin Esslin"), in his *The Theatre of the Absurd* (1960), argued that *Waiting for Godot* was part of a broader literary movement he called the [Theatre of the Absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Absurd "Theatre of the Absurd"), a form of theatre that stemmed from the absurdist philosophy of [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus"). Absurdism itself is a branch of existentialism, pioneered by [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Kierkegaard "Søren Kierkegaard"), and posits that, while inherent meaning might exist, human beings are incapable of finding it due to some form of mental or philosophical limitation. Thus, humanity is doomed to be faced with *the Absurd*, or the absolute absurdity of the existence in lack of intrinsic purpose.[^83]

#### Ethical

Just after Didi and Gogo have been particularly selfish and callous, the boy comes to say that Godot is not coming. The boy (or pair of boys) may be seen to represent meekness and hope before compassion is consciously excluded by an evolving personality and character, in which case they may be the youthful Pozzo and Lucky. Thus Godot is compassion and fails to arrive every day, as he says he will. No-one is concerned that a boy is beaten.[^84] In this interpretation, there is the irony that only by changing their hearts to be compassionate can the characters fixed to the tree move on and cease to have to wait for Godot.

### Christian

Much of the play is steeped in scriptural allusion. The boy from Act I mentions that he and his brother mind Godot's [sheep and goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sheep_and_the_Goats "The Sheep and the Goats"). Much can be read into Beckett's inclusion of the story of the two thieves from [Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Luke "Gospel of Luke") 23:39–43 [^85] and the ensuing discussion of repentance. It is easy to see the tree as the [Christian cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross "Christian cross") or the [tree of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_life "Tree of life"). Some see God and Godot as one and the same. Vladimir's "Christ have mercy upon us!" [^86] could be taken as evidence that at least he believes so.

Another, perhaps less conspicuous, potentially religious element is Pozzo's bout with blindness, during which he comes to resemble the biblical figure of Bartimaeus (['The Blind Beggar'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healing_the_blind_near_Jericho "Healing the blind near Jericho")).

This reading is given further weight early in the first act when Estragon asks Vladimir what he has requested from Godot:[^87]

*Vladimir*: Oh... nothing very definite.  
*Estragon*: A kind of prayer.  
*Vladimir*: Precisely.  
*Estragon*: A vague supplication.  
*Vladimir*: Exactly.

Other explicit Christian elements include [repentance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repentance "Repentance"),[^88] the [Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel "Gospel"),[^89] a [Saviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah "Messiah"),[^90] human beings made in [God's image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_of_God "Image of God"),[^91] the [cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross "Christian cross"),[^92] and [Cain and Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain_and_Abel "Cain and Abel").[^93]

According to biographer [Anthony Cronin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Cronin "Anthony Cronin"), Beckett "always possessed a Bible, at the end more than one edition, and Bible [concordances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordance_\(publishing\) "Concordance (publishing)") were always among the reference books on his shelves." [^94] Beckett said: "Christianity is a mythology with which I am perfectly familiar so I naturally use it." [^95] Cronin argues the biblical references "may be ironic or even sarcastic".[^96]

"In answer to a defence counsel question in 1937 (during the [libel action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Was_Going_Down_Sackville_Street#Libel_lawsuit "As I Was Going Down Sackville Street") brought by his uncle against [Oliver St. John Gogarty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_St._John_Gogarty "Oliver St. John Gogarty")) as to whether he was a Christian, Jew or atheist, Beckett replied, 'None of the three'".[^97] Mary Bryden wrote, "the hypothesised God who emerges from Beckett's texts is one who is both cursed for his perverse absence and cursed for his surveillant presence. He is by turns dismissed, satirised, or ignored, but he, and his tortured son, are never definitively discarded." [^98]

### Autobiographical

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Friedrich_-_Two_Men_Contemplating_the_Moon.jpg)

According to Beckett, Caspar David Friedrich 's painting Two Men Contemplating the Moon was an inspiration for the play

*Waiting for Godot* has been described as a "metaphor for the long walk into [Roussillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roussillon "Roussillon"), when Beckett and [Suzanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzanne_Dechevaux-Dumesnil "Suzanne Dechevaux-Dumesnil") slept in haystacks... during the day and walked by night... \[or\] of the relationship of Beckett to [Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce") ".[^99] Beckett told [Ruby Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_Cohn "Ruby Cohn") that [Caspar David Friedrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspar_David_Friedrich "Caspar David Friedrich") 's painting *[Two Men Contemplating the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Men_Contemplating_the_Moon "Two Men Contemplating the Moon")*, which he saw on his journey to Germany in 1936, was the source for the play.[^100]

### Sexual

Though the sexuality of Vladimir and Estragon is not always considered by critics,[^101] [^102] some see the two vagabonds as an ageing homosexual couple, who are worn out, with broken spirits, impotent and not engaging sexually any longer. The two appear to be written as a parody of a married couple.[^103] Peter Boxall points out that the play features two characters who seem to have shared life together for years; they quarrel, embrace, and are mutually dependent.[^104] Beckett was interviewed at the time the play was premiering in New York, and, speaking of his writings and characters in general, Beckett said "I'm working with impotence, ignorance. I don't think impotence has been exploited in the past." [^105]

Pozzo and his slave, Lucky, arrive on the scene. Pozzo is a stout man, who wields a whip and holds a rope around Lucky's neck. Some critics have considered that the relationship of these two characters is homosexual and sado-masochistic in nature.[^106] Lucky's long speech is a torrent of broken ideas and speculations regarding man, sex, God, and time. It has been said that the play contains little or no sexual hope, this being the play's lament, and the source of the play's humour and comedic tenderness.[^107] [Norman Mailer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Mailer "Norman Mailer") wonders if Beckett might be restating the sexual and moral basis of Christianity, that life and strength is found in an adoration of those in the lower depths where God is concealed.[^108]

### Beckett's objection to the casting of female actors

Beckett was not open to most interpretative approaches to his work. He famously objected when, in the 1980s, several women's acting companies began to stage the play. "Women don't have [prostates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostate "Prostate") ", said Beckett,[^109] a reference to the fact that Vladimir frequently has to leave the stage to urinate. [Florian Illies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florian_Illies "Florian Illies") speculates that Beckett's objection to the casting of women originates in the painting *[Man and Woman Contemplating the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Men_Contemplating_the_Moon#Man_and_Woman_Contemplating_the_Moon "Two Men Contemplating the Moon")* by Caspar David Friedrich: Friedrich's companion piece *[Two Men Contemplating the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Men_Contemplating_the_Moon "Two Men Contemplating the Moon")* was the inspiration for Beckett's play, and apparently he was taken aback when, only later, did he learn that the painting was one of a complementary pair, where the other work's subjects were a man and a woman.[^100]

In 1988 a Dutch theatre company, De Haarlemse Toneelschuur, put on a production directed by Matin Van Veldhuizen with all female actors, using a French-to-Dutch translation by [Jacoba Van Velde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacoba_van_Velde "Jacoba van Velde").[^110] Beckett brought an unsuccessful lawsuit against the theatre company. "The issue of gender seemed to him to be so vital a distinction for a playwright to make that he reacted angrily, instituting a ban on all productions of his plays in The Netherlands." [^111] This ban was short-lived, however.

In 1991 (two years after Beckett's death), a French judge ruled that productions with female casts would not cause excessive damage to Beckett's legacy, and allowed the play to be performed by the all-female cast of the Brut de Beton theater company at the [Avignon Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival_d'Avignon "Festival d'Avignon"), although an objection by Beckett's representative had to be read before each performance.[^112]

Similarly, an injunction was issued against a theatre in Pontedera, [Tuscany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscany "Tuscany"), by lawyers for [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett") 's estate who did not want female actors to play Vladimir and Estragon in the play, but in 2006 a court in Rome ruled that the women could play the roles.[^113]

At the 1995 [Acco Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acco_Festival_of_Alternative_Israeli_Theatre "Acco Festival of Alternative Israeli Theatre"), director [Nola Chilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nola_Chilton "Nola Chilton") staged a production with Daniella Michaeli in the role of Lucky.[^114] In 2021, [São João National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A3o_Jo%C3%A3o_National_Theatre "São João National Theatre") in Porto, Portugal, staged a version with Maria Leite as Lucky.[^115]

The topic of Beckett and his estate preventing female productions of the play is explored in the play *Godot Is a Woman*, which was staged at the [Edinburgh Festival Fringe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_Festival_Fringe "Edinburgh Festival Fringe") in 2023 [^116] by the theatre company Silent Faces.[^117]

## Production history

### 1950s to 1969

#### France and Germany

"\[O\]n 17 February 1952... an abridged version of the play was performed in the studio of the [Club d'Essai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_d'Essai "Club d'Essai") de la Radio and was broadcast on \[French\] radio... \[A\]lthough he sent a polite note that [Roger Blin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blin "Roger Blin") read out, Beckett himself did not turn up." [^118] Part of his introduction reads:

> I don't know who Godot is. I don't even know (above all don't know) if he exists. And I don't know if they believe in him or not – those two who are waiting for him. The other two who pass by towards the end of each of the two acts, that must be to break up the monotony. All I knew I showed. It's not much, but it's enough for me, by a wide margin. I'll even say that I would have been satisfied with less. As for wanting to find in all that a broader, loftier meaning to carry away from the performance, along with the program and the [Eskimo pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo_pie "Eskimo pie"), I cannot see the point of it. But it must be possible... [Estragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estragon "Estragon"), [Vladimir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Vladimir (Waiting for Godot)"), [Pozzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozzo_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Pozzo (Waiting for Godot)"), [Lucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucky_\(Waiting_for_Godot\) "Lucky (Waiting for Godot)"), their time and their space, I was able to know them a little, but far from the need to understand. Maybe they owe you explanations. Let them supply it. Without me. They and I are through with each other.[^119]

The play was first published in September 1952 by [Les Éditions de Minuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_%C3%89ditions_de_Minuit "Les Éditions de Minuit") [^120] [^121] and released on 17 October 1952 in advance of the first full theatrical performance;[^122] only 2500 copies were printed of this first edition.[^123] On 4 January 1953, "\[t\]hirty reviewers came to the *générale* of *En attendant Godot* before the public opening... Contrary to later legend, the reviewers were kind... Some dozen reviews in daily newspapers range\[d\] from tolerant to enthusiastic... Reviews in the weeklies \[were\] longer and more fervent; moreover, they appeared in time to lure spectators to that first thirty-day run" [^124] which began on 5 January 1953 at the Théâtre de Babylone, Paris. Early public performances were not, however, without incident: during one performance "the curtain had to be brought down after Lucky's [monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monologue "Monologue") as twenty, well-dressed, but disgruntled spectators whistled and hooted derisively... One of the protesters \[even\] wrote a vituperative letter dated 2 February 1953 to *[Le Monde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Monde "Le Monde")*." [^125]

The cast comprised [Pierre Latour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Latour_\(actor\)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Pierre Latour (actor) (page does not exist)") (Estragon), [Lucien Raimbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucien_Raimbourg "Lucien Raimbourg") (Vladimir), [Jean Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Martin "Jean Martin") (Lucky) and [Roger Blin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blin "Roger Blin") (Pozzo). The actor due to play Pozzo found a more remunerative role and so the director – a shy, lean man in real life – had to step in and play the stout bombaster himself with a pillow amplifying his stomach. Both boys were played by Serge Lecointe. The entire production was done on the thinnest of shoestring budgets; the large battered valise that Martin carried "was found among the city's refuse by the husband of the [theatre dresser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresser_\(theatre\) "Dresser (theatre)") on his rounds as he worked clearing the dustbins",[^126] for example. Blin helped the actors embody their characters by asking them to determine a physical malady that would contribute to the nature of their character.[^127] Latour emphasized Estragon's bad feet and Raimbourg Vladimir's prostate problems, while Blin played Pozzo as a man with heart difficulties. Martin played Lucky with the symptoms of [Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease "Parkinson's disease").[^128]

A particularly significant production – from Beckett's perspective – took place in [Lüttringhausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCttringhausen "Lüttringhausen") Prison near [Remscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remscheid "Remscheid") in Germany. An inmate obtained a copy of the French [first edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_edition "First edition"), translated it himself into German and obtained permission to stage the play. The first night had been on 29 November 1953. He wrote to Beckett in October 1954: "You will be surprised to be receiving a letter about your play *Waiting for Godot*, from a prison where so many [thieves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theft "Theft"), [forgers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forgery "Forgery"), [toughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal "Criminal"), [homos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality "Homosexuality"), [crazy men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunatic "Lunatic") and [killers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder "Murder") spend this bitch of a life waiting... and waiting... and waiting. Waiting for what? Godot? Perhaps." [^129] Beckett was intensely moved and intended to visit the prison to see a last performance of the play but it never happened. This marked "the beginning of Beckett's enduring links with prisons and prisoners... He took a tremendous interest in productions of his plays performed in prisons." [^130]

#### UK

Like all of Beckett's translations, the English translation of *Waiting for Godot* is not simply a literal translation of **En attendant Godot**. "Small but significant differences separate the French and English text. Some, like Vladimir's inability to remember the farmer's name (Bonnelly [^131]), show how the translation became more indefinite, attrition and loss of memory more pronounced." [^132] A number of biographical details were removed, all adding to a general "vaguening" [^133] of the text which he continued to trim for the rest of his life.

The English-language version saw its first UK production on 3 August 1955 at the [Arts Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_Theatre "Arts Theatre") in London, directed by [Peter Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hall_\(director\) "Peter Hall (director)"), with [Paul Daneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Daneman "Paul Daneman") as Vladimir, [Peter Woodthorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Woodthorpe "Peter Woodthorpe") as Estragon, [Peter Bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Bull "Peter Bull") as Pozzo, [Timothy Bateson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Bateson "Timothy Bateson") as Lucky, and Michael Walker as The Boy; when the play transferred to the [Criterion Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_Theatre "Criterion Theatre") on 12 September, [Hugh Burden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Burden "Hugh Burden") took over the role of Vladimir.[^134] During an early rehearsal Hall told the cast "I haven't really the foggiest idea what some of it means... But if we stop and discuss every line we'll never open." [^135]

Again, the printed version preceded it (New York: Grove Press, 1954) but Faber's "mutilated" edition did not materialise until 1956. A "corrected" edition was subsequently produced in 1965. "The most accurate text is in *Theatrical Notebooks* I, (Ed.) Dougald McMillan and James Knowlson (Faber and Grove, 1993). It is based on Beckett's revisions for his Schiller-Theater production (1975) and the London San Quentin Drama Workshop, based on the Schiller production but revised further at the [Riverside Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside_Studios "Riverside Studios") (March 1984)." [^136]

In the 1950s, theatre was strictly [censored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship "Censorship") in the UK, to Beckett's amazement since he thought it a bastion of [free speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech"). The [Lord Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain "Lord Chamberlain") insisted that the word " [erection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erection "Erection") " be removed, "'Fartov' became 'Popov' and Mrs Gozzo had ' [warts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wart "Wart") ' instead of ' [clap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonorrhea "Gonorrhea") '".[^137] Indeed, there were attempts to ban the play completely. Lady Dorothy Howitt wrote to the Lord Chamberlain, saying: "One of the many themes running through the play is the desire of two old tramps continually to relieve themselves. Such a dramatisation of lavatory necessities is offensive and against all sense of British decency." [^138] "The first unexpurgated version of *Godot* in England... opened at the [Royal Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre") on 30 December 1964." [^139]

The London run was not without incident. In 1959, Peter Bull recalled the reaction of the first-night audience:

> Waves of hostility came whirling over the footlights, and the mass exodus, which was to form such a feature of the run of the piece, started quite soon after the curtain had risen. The audible groans were also fairly disconcerting... The curtain fell to mild applause, we took a scant three calls \[Peter Woodthorpe reported only one [curtain call](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_call "Curtain call")\] [^140] and a depression and a sense of anti-climax descended on us all.[^141]

The critics were less than kind but "\[e\]verything changed on Sunday 7 August 1955 with [Kenneth Tynan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Tynan "Kenneth Tynan") 's and [Harold Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Hobson "Harold Hobson") 's reviews in *[The Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Observer "The Observer")* and *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times "The Sunday Times")*. Beckett was always grateful to the two reviewers for their support... which more or less transformed the play overnight into the rage of London." [^142] "At the end of the year, the [Evening Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evening_Standard "Evening Standard") Drama Awards were held for the first time... Feelings ran high and the opposition, led by Sir [Malcolm Sargent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Sargent "Malcolm Sargent"), threatened to resign if *Godot* won \[The Best New Play category\]. An English compromise was worked out by changing the title of the award. *Godot* became The Most Controversial Play of the Year. It is a prize that has never been given since." [^143]

On 27 April 1960, the [BBC Third Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Third_Programme "BBC Third Programme") broadcast the very first radio adaptation, directed by [Donald McWhinnie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_McWhinnie "Donald McWhinnie"), with [Patrick Magee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Magee_\(actor\) "Patrick Magee (actor)") as Vladimir, [Wilfrid Brambell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Brambell "Wilfrid Brambell") as Estragon, [Felix Felton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Felton "Felix Felton") as Pozzo, [Donal Donnelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donal_Donnelly "Donal Donnelly") as Lucky and Jeremy Ward as The Boy.[^144]

On 26 June 1961, Donald McWhinnie directed a production broadcast on [BBC Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television "BBC Television"), with [Jack MacGowran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_MacGowran "Jack MacGowran") as Vladimir, [Peter Woodthorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Woodthorpe "Peter Woodthorpe") as Estragon, [Felix Felton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Felton "Felix Felton") as Pozzo, [Timothy Bateson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Bateson "Timothy Bateson") as Lucky and Mark Mileham as The Boy.[^145]

On 5 February 1962, the [BBC Home Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Home_Service "BBC Home Service") broadcast a radio production as part of the *From the Fifties* series, directed by [Robin Midgley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Midgley "Robin Midgley") with [Nigel Stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Stock_\(actor\) "Nigel Stock (actor)") as Vladimir, [Kenneth Griffith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Griffith "Kenneth Griffith") as Estragon, Philip Leaver as Pozzo, [Andrew Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Sachs "Andrew Sachs") as Lucky and Terry Raven as The Boy.[^146]

In December 1964, [Nicol Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicol_Williamson "Nicol Williamson") played Vladimir, [Alfred Lynch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Lynch "Alfred Lynch") played Estragon and [Jack MacGowran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_MacGowran "Jack MacGowran") played Lucky in a production at London's [Royal Court Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre") directed by [Anthony Page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Page "Anthony Page"). This was the first West End revival since the play's British première.

#### US

Planning for an American tour for *Waiting for Godot* started in 1955. The first American tour was directed by Alan Schneider and produced by Michael Myerberg. [Bert Lahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bert_Lahr "Bert Lahr") and [Tom Ewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Ewell "Tom Ewell") acted in the initial production.

The first part of the tour was a disaster. The play was originally set to be shown in Washington and Philadelphia. However, low advance sales forced the play to be performed in Miami for two weeks in early January 1956 at the newly opened [Coconut Grove Playhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove_Playhouse "Coconut Grove Playhouse"), where the audience was made up of vacationers.[^147] It had been promoted as "the laugh sensation of two continents" in the notices run by Myerberg in the local newspapers.[^147]

Most audience members were baffled by the play.[^148] [^149] [^150] [^151] [^152] [^153] [^154] Theatregoers would leave after the first act, describing it as a play where "nothing happens", and taxi drivers would wait in front of the theatre to take them home.[^155] [^156] The Miami showing caused the cancellation of the showings in New York.

By April 1956, new showings were planned. That month, Schneider and most of the cast were replaced. [Herbert Berghof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Berghof "Herbert Berghof") took over as director and [E. G. Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._G._Marshall "E. G. Marshall") replaced Tom Ewell as Vladimir.[^157] The play had its [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre "Broadway theatre") premiere at the [John Golden Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Golden_Theatre "John Golden Theatre") on 19 April 1956, with [Bert Lahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bert_Lahr "Bert Lahr") as Estragon, [E. G. Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._G._Marshall "E. G. Marshall") as Vladimir, [Alvin Epstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Epstein "Alvin Epstein") as Lucky, and [Kurt Kasznar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Kasznar "Kurt Kasznar") as Pozzo.[^158] The New York showing of the play prompted discussions of the play being an allegory. One reviewer, [Henry Hewes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Hewes_\(critic\) "Henry Hewes (critic)") of the *[Saturday Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturday_Review_\(U.S._magazine\) "Saturday Review (U.S. magazine)")*, identified Godot as God, Pozzo as a capitalist-aristocrat, and Lucky as labour-proletarian.[^157] This prompted Beckett to issue a rare statement, stating that the reaction was based on a misconception of the play. To Beckett, the play tries not to be able to be defined.[^159] The New York showing of the play was well-received with critics. [Brooks Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooks_Atkinson "Brooks Atkinson") of *The New York Times* praised Lahr for his performance as Estragon.[^160] The production was recorded as a two-record album by [Columbia Masterworks Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Masterworks_Records "Columbia Masterworks Records").[^161]

In 1957, four years after its world premiere, *Waiting for Godot* was staged for one night only at the [San Quentin State Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Quentin_State_Prison "San Quentin State Prison") in California. [Herbert Blau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Blau "Herbert Blau") with the San Francisco [Actor's Workshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor's_Workshop "Actor's Workshop") directed the production. Some 1,400 inmates encountered the performance.[^162] Beckett later gave [Rick Cluchey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Cluchey "Rick Cluchey"), a former prisoner from San Quentin, financial and moral support over a period of many years.[^130] Cluchey played Vladimir in two productions in the former [Gallows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallows "Gallows") room of the San Quentin California State Prison, which had been converted into a 65-seat theatre and, like the German prisoner before him, went on to work on a variety of Beckett's plays after his release. Cluchey said, "The thing that everyone in San Quentin understood about Beckett, while the rest of the world had trouble catching up, was what it meant to be in the face of it." [^163] The attitude of this troupe was to move it away from a commercial attitude to an *[avant garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant_garde "Avant garde")* attitude.[^164] As well, the play did not have competition between the actors playing Vladimir and Estragon for being the star of the show.[^165] The most successful showing was in November 1957 at the San Quentin prison, where the play had a profound impact on the inmates and spurred them to start a drama group in the prison. They would go on to produce seven of Beckett's works.[^166] In 1958, the play, produced by the San Francisco Actor's Workshop, would be chosen to go to Brussels for the [1958 World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1958_World's_Fair "1958 World's Fair").[^167]

The first Broadway revival was produced in 1957 at the [Ethel Barrymore Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethel_Barrymore_Theatre "Ethel Barrymore Theatre") directed by [Herbert Berghof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Berghof "Herbert Berghof"), but only ran for six performances (21–26 January).[^168] It had an all-Black cast, including [Earle Hyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earle_Hyman "Earle Hyman") as Vladimir, [Mantan Moreland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantan_Moreland "Mantan Moreland") as Estragon, [Rex Ingram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rex_Ingram_\(actor\) "Rex Ingram (actor)") as Pozzo and [Geoffrey Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Holder "Geoffrey Holder") as Lucky. This rendition of *Waiting for Godot* played on themes of the [Africana absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Surrealism "Afro-Surrealism") as opposed to the [European absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_absurd "Theatre of the absurd"). For example, Hyman's career as a classical actor and Moreland's as a vaudeville actor were used to juxtapose the different facets of African American theatre in the mind of the audience.[^169]

Also in May 1957, a production directed by Walter Biakel was staged at the [Studebaker Theatre in Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_Arts_Building_\(Chicago\) "Fine Arts Building (Chicago)") with [Harvey Korman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Korman "Harvey Korman") as Vladimir, [Louis Zorich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Zorich "Louis Zorich") as Estragon, [Moultrie Patten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moultrie_Patten "Moultrie Patten") as Pozzo and [Mike Nichols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Nichols "Mike Nichols") as Lucky.[^170]

In 1965, a production at the [Olney Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olney_Theatre_Center "Olney Theatre Center") in [Olney, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olney,_Maryland "Olney, Maryland"), starred [Dana Elcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Elcar "Dana Elcar") as "Vladimir" and [Stefan Gierasch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefan_Gierasch "Stefan Gierasch") as "Estragon" as part of *A Festival of the Absurd*.

#### Australia

In the Australian premiere at the Arrow Theatre in Melbourne in 1957, [Barry Humphries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Humphries "Barry Humphries") played Estragon opposite [Peter O'Shaughnessy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_O'Shaughnessy "Peter O'Shaughnessy") 's Vladimir.[^171]

#### Canada

*Waiting for Godot* was first performed at the [Stratford Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford_Festival "Stratford Festival") in 1968 at the Avon Theatre in a production directed by [William Hutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hutt_\(actor\) "William Hutt (actor)"), with Powys Thomas as Vladimir, Eric Donkin as Estragon, [James Blendick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Blendick "James Blendick") as Pozzo, Adrian Pecknold as Lucky and Douglas Birkenshaw as The Boy.[^172]

#### South Africa

The very first South African production was performed in 1955 at the [Little Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Theatre_\(Cape_Town\) "Little Theatre (Cape Town)") in [Cape Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town "Cape Town"), produced by Leonard Schach, with Gavin Haughton as Vladimir, Alec Bell as Estragon, Donald Inskip as Lucky, Gordon Roberts as Pozzo and Frank Rothgiesser as The Boy.[^173] The play was also presented at the Hofmeyr Theatre and then taken on a tour of several country towns in South Africa.

#### Brazil

After a few amateur productions in the 1950s, the first professional staging of the play in Brazil happened in 1969, directed by Flávio Rangel and staged by actress [Cacilda Becker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacilda_Becker "Cacilda Becker") as Estragon and her real-life husband, actor [Walmor Chagas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walmor_Chagas "Walmor Chagas") as Vladimir. After few performances, on 6 May 1969, Becker had a [stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke "Stroke") and collapsed during the intermission. She was immediately taken to a hospital, still wearing the play's costume, and remained in a [coma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coma "Coma") for 38 days until her death on 14 June.[^174]

#### Poland

The Polish premiere took place on 25 January 1957, at Teatr Współczesny in Warsaw. directed by Jerzy Kreczmar and featuring [Tadeusz Fijewski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadeusz_Fijewski "Tadeusz Fijewski") as Vladimir and [Józef Kondrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Kondrat "Józef Kondrat") as Estragon.[^175] The Polish premiere was the fifth in the world and the first in the Communist Bloc.[^176]

### 1970s to 2000

Set of Theatre Royal Haymarket 2009 production

In 1977, [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_System "Public Broadcasting System") broadcast an adaptation for television directed by [Charles S. Dubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_S._Dubin "Charles S. Dubin") and performed by the [Los Angeles Actors' Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Actors'_Theatre "Los Angeles Actors' Theatre"), with [Dana Elcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Elcar "Dana Elcar") as Vladimir, [Donald Moffat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Moffat "Donald Moffat") as Estragon, [Ralph Waite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Waite "Ralph Waite") as Pozzo and [Bruce French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_French_\(actor\) "Bruce French (actor)") as Lucky.[^177]

On 4 September 1977, as part of the British television series *Drama*, the [Open University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_University "Open University") filmed a production of *Godot* directed by Richard Callanan with [Leo McKern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_McKern "Leo McKern") as Estragon, [Max Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Wall "Max Wall") as Vladimir, [Graham Crowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Crowden "Graham Crowden") as Pozzo, Basil Clarke as Lucky and Toby Page as The Boy.[^178]

In 1978, a production was staged by [Walter Asmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Asmus "Walter Asmus") at the [Brooklyn Academy of Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Academy_of_Music "Brooklyn Academy of Music") in New York City with [Sam Waterston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Waterston "Sam Waterston") as Vladimir, [Austin Pendleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Pendleton "Austin Pendleton") as Estragon, [Milo O'Shea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milo_O'Shea "Milo O'Shea") as Lucky and Michael Egan as Pozzo.[^179]

A young [Geoffrey Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Rush "Geoffrey Rush") played Vladimir opposite his then flatmate [Mel Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mel_Gibson "Mel Gibson") as Estragon in 1979 at the Jane Street Theatre in Sydney.[^180]

In 1980, [Braham Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braham_Murray "Braham Murray") directed a production at the [Royal Exchange Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_Manchester "Royal Exchange, Manchester") in [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester "Manchester") with [Max Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Wall "Max Wall") as Vladimir, [Trevor Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trevor_Peacock "Trevor Peacock") as Estragon and [Wolfe Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfe_Morris "Wolfe Morris") as Pozzo.[^181]

Also in 1980, a production was performed at the [Baxter Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baxter_Theatre_Centre "Baxter Theatre Centre") in Cape Town, directed by [Donald Howarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Howarth "Donald Howarth"), with [John Kani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kani "John Kani") ("Vladimir"), [Winston Ntshona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Ntshona "Winston Ntshona") ("Estragon"), [Pieter-Dirk Uys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter-Dirk_Uys "Pieter-Dirk Uys") ("Pozzo"), Peter Piccolo ("Lucky") and [Silamour Philander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soli_Philander "Soli Philander") ("The Boy").[^173] The multiracial cast, approved by Beckett himself, caused quite a stir, but the play received good reviews. After the Cape Town run, the play was also performed at the [Grahamstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makhanda,_South_Africa "Makhanda, South Africa") National Arts Festival, the [Port Elizabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gqeberha "Gqeberha") [Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Elizabeth_Opera_House "Port Elizabeth Opera House"), and the [Market Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Theatre_\(Johannesburg\) "Market Theatre (Johannesburg)"), Johannesburg (where it ran for three weeks). In 1981, the production went on an international tour to the US and Britain, with [Bill Flynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Flynn "Bill Flynn") replacing Uys as "Pozzo". The tour included New Haven, Connecticut, [The Old Vic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Vic "The Old Vic") Theatre in London and the [Oxford Playhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Playhouse "Oxford Playhouse") in Oxford. It was also invited to participate in the First International Baltimore Theatre Festival but on arrival the play was picketed by anti-Apartheid demonstrators who claimed that it and the Baxter Theatre were "part and parcel of the South African propaganda machine to misrepresent what was taking place in the country", so the performances were canceled.

The 1984 [Stratford Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford_Festival "Stratford Festival") production of *Waiting for Godot*, directed by Leon Rubin, was performed at the Tom Patterson Theatre, with [Brian Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Bedford "Brian Bedford") as Vladimir, Edward Abenza as Estragon, [Andreas Katsulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Katsulas "Andreas Katsulas") as Pozzo, Paul Zimet as Lucky and Adam Poynter as The Boy.[^182]

The Mitzi E. Newhouse Theater at [Lincoln Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center "Lincoln Center") was the site of a 1988 revival directed by [Mike Nichols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Nichols "Mike Nichols"), featuring [Robin Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Williams "Robin Williams") (Estragon), [Steve Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Martin "Steve Martin") (Vladimir), [Bill Irwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Irwin "Bill Irwin") (Lucky), [F. Murray Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Murray_Abraham "F. Murray Abraham") (Pozzo), and [Lukas Haas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lukas_Haas "Lukas Haas") (boy). With a limited run of seven weeks and an all-star cast, it was financially successful,[^183] but the critical reception was not particularly favourable, with [Frank Rich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Rich "Frank Rich") of *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* writing, "Audiences will still be waiting for a transcendent *Godot* long after the clowns at Lincoln Center, like so many others passing through Beckett's eternal universe before them, have come and gone." [^184]

The play was revived in [London's West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre "West End theatre") at the [Queen's Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sondheim_Theatre "Sondheim Theatre") in a production directed by [Les Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Blair "Les Blair"), which opened on 30 September 1991. [Rik Mayall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rik_Mayall "Rik Mayall") played Vladimir and [Adrian Edmondson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Edmondson "Adrian Edmondson") played Estragon, with [Philip Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Jackson_\(actor\) "Philip Jackson (actor)") as Pozzo and [Christopher Ryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Ryan "Christopher Ryan") as Lucky; the boy was played by [Dean Gaffney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Gaffney "Dean Gaffney") and Duncan Thornley. [Derek Jarman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Jarman "Derek Jarman") provided the scenic design, in collaboration with Madeleine Morris.[^185]

In 1992, in what would be his last stage appearance, [Dana Elcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Elcar "Dana Elcar") reprised his 1965 stage role and 1977 TV movie role of Vladimir in a 1992 Los Angeles production at the Santa Paula Theatre Center directed by [Deborah LaVine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deborah_LaVine "Deborah LaVine").[^186]

On 3 September 1994, a rare French-language recording of the play, recorded at the Theatre de Babylone shortly after the 1953 Paris premiere with the original cast and director *(see [above](#France_and_Germany))* was broadcast on [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3 "BBC Radio 3").[^187] The following day, 4 September 1994, [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3 "BBC Radio 3") broadcast a production in English with [Alan Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Howard_\(actor\) "Alan Howard (actor)") as Vladimir, [Michael Maloney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Maloney "Michael Maloney") as Estragon, Stratford Jones as Pozzo, [Simon Russell Beale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Russell_Beale "Simon Russell Beale") as Lucky, Tristan Moriarty as The Boy and [Geraldine McEwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geraldine_McEwan "Geraldine McEwan") as The Narrator;[^188] this production was rebroadcast on BBC Radio 3 30 June 1995 and 5 September 1999.

In 1996, the [Stratford Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford_Festival "Stratford Festival") staged a production directed by [Brian Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Bedford "Brian Bedford"), with [Stephen Ouimette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Ouimette "Stephen Ouimette") as Estragon, [Tom McCamus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_McCamus "Tom McCamus") as Vladimir, [James Blendick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Blendick "James Blendick") as Pozzo, Tim MacDonald as Lucky and [Joe Dinicol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Dinicol "Joe Dinicol") as The Boy.[^189] The cast reunited in March 1997 to perform the play on [CBC Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBC_Radio "CBC Radio") 's "Bank of Montreal Stratford Festival Series" and again at the Stratford Festival for the 1998 season at the Tom Patterson Theatre (with Philip Psutka replacing Dinicol as The Boy), again directed by Bedford.[^190]

In June 1999, the [Royal Exchange, Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_Manchester "Royal Exchange, Manchester") staged a production directed by Matthew Lloyd with [Richard Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wilson_\(Scottish_actor\) "Richard Wilson (Scottish actor)") as Vladimir, [Brian Pettifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Pettifer "Brian Pettifer") as Estragon and [Nicky Henson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicky_Henson "Nicky Henson") as Pozzo.[^191]

### 2000 to present

[Neil Armfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Armfield "Neil Armfield") directed a controversial production in January 2003 with [Max Cullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Cullen "Max Cullen") as Estragon, [John Gaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gaden "John Gaden") as Vladimir, Boddan Koca as Pozzo and [Steve Le Marquand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Le_Marquand "Steve Le Marquand") as Lucky at Sydney's [Belvoir St Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belvoir_St_Theatre "Belvoir St Theatre").[^192] A representative of Beckett's estate was present at opening night and had believed a contract for the play had stated that no music was to be used in the production.[^193]

On 16 April 2006, BBC Radio 3 broadcast a production directed by [John Tydeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tydeman "John Tydeman"), with [Sean Barrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_Barrett_\(actor\) "Sean Barrett (actor)") as Vladimir, [David Burke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Burke_\(British_actor\) "David Burke (British actor)") as Estragon, [Nigel Anthony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Anthony "Nigel Anthony") as Lucky and The Narrator, [Terence Rigby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Rigby "Terence Rigby") as Pozzo and Zachary Fox as The Boy.[^194]

On 2 and 3 November 2007, two performances were staged in the [Lower Ninth Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Ninth_Ward "Lower Ninth Ward") of [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans"), two years after the neighborhood had been devastated by the [failure of the federal levee system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_Hurricane_Katrina_in_New_Orleans "Effects of Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans") caused by [Hurricane Katrina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Katrina "Hurricane Katrina"). This was followed by two performances in the similarly damaged neighborhood [Gentilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentilly,_New_Orleans "Gentilly, New Orleans") on 9 and 10 November. The production was staged by American artist [Paul Chan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Chan_\(artist\) "Paul Chan (artist)"), the NYC-based arts organization [Creative Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Time "Creative Time"), and the [Classical Theatre of Harlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Theatre_of_Harlem "Classical Theatre of Harlem"). It featured New Orleans native [Wendell Pierce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendell_Pierce "Wendell Pierce") as Vladimir, J. Kyle Manzay as Estragon, Tony Felix and Michael Pepp as Boy.[^195] [^196] [Waiting for Godot in New Orleans](https://creativetime.org/programs/archive/2007/chan/play.html)

## Adaptations

Beckett received numerous requests to adapt *Waiting for Godot* for film and television.[^215] The author, however, resisted these offers, except for occasional approval out of friendship or sympathy for the person making the request. This was the case when he agreed to some televised productions in his lifetime (including a [1961 American telecast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Play_of_the_Week "The Play of the Week") with [Zero Mostel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_Mostel "Zero Mostel") as Estragon and [Burgess Meredith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgess_Meredith "Burgess Meredith") as Vladimir that *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times "New York Times")* theatre critic [Alvin Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Klein "Alvin Klein") describes as having "left critics bewildered and is now a classic").[^135] When Keep Films made Beckett an offer to film an adaptation in which [Peter O'Toole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_O'Toole "Peter O'Toole") would feature, Beckett tersely told his French publisher to advise them: "I do not want a film of *Godot*." [^216] The [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") broadcast a television production of *Waiting for Godot* on 26 June 1961 *(see [above](#UK))*, a version for radio having already been transmitted on 25 April 1960. Beckett watched the programme with a few close friends in Peter Woodthorpe's [Chelsea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea,_London "Chelsea, London") flat. He was unhappy with what he saw. "My play", he said, "wasn't written for this box. My play was written for small men locked in a big space. Here you're all too big for the place." [^217] One analysis argued that Beckett's opposition to alterations and creative adaptations stem from his abiding concern with audience reaction rather than proprietary rights over a text being performed.[^218]

On the other hand, theatrical adaptations have had more success. For instance, Andre Engel adapted the play in 1979 and was produced in Strasbourg. In this performance, the two main characters were fragmented into 10 characters. The first four involved Gogo, Didi, Lucky, and Pozzo while the rest were divided into three pairs: two tramps, a pair of grim heterosexuals, and a bride raped by her groom.[^219] Each of these embodied some characteristics of Estragon and Vladimir. A similar approach was employed by Tamiya Kuriyama who directed his own adaptation of the play in Tokyo. These interpretations, which only used extracts from the dialogues of the original, focused on the minds of the urban-dwellers today, who are considered to be no longer individuals but one of the many or of the whole, which turned such individuals into machines.[^219]

In 1991, Chinese avant-garde theatre director Meng Jinghui (b. 1964) adapted the play (*Dengdai Geduo* in Chinese) for his master's graduation performance at Central Academy of Drama, with unmistakable allusions to the [Tiananmen Square protests in 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre "1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre"). By using metatheatrical tropes, Meng's adaptation not only condemned both the government and the protesters but expressed strong senses of disillusion and cynicism.[^220] Additionally, by dramatically changing the main characters and the ending—transforming the philosophical and sceptic Vladimir in the original script into a vengeful idealist-turned-cynic who strangled Godot to death, Meng captures the conflicted feelings of the Chinese generation born in the 1960s towards China's socialist past.[^221]

Arshad Mushtaq directed a 2004 Kashmiri adaptation of the play titled *Su Yee* (transl. He will come) staged at Tagore Hall, [Srinagar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinagar "Srinagar").[^222] [^223] The play won best direction and production by the Jammu and Kashmir government's Academy of Art, Culture and Languages in 2004. In 2005, Mushtaq won the best director award at [Doordarshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doordarshan "Doordarshan") 's theatre festival.[^222] [^224]

A web series adaptation titled *[While Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/While_Waiting_for_Godot "While Waiting for Godot")* was also produced at [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University "New York University") in 2013, setting the story among the modern-day [New York homeless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homelessness_in_New_York "Homelessness in New York"). Directed by Rudi Azank, the English script was based on Beckett's original French manuscript of **En attendant Godot** (the new title being an alternate translation of the French) prior to censorship from British publishing houses in the 1950s, as well as adaptation to the stage. Season 1 of the web series won Best Cinematography at the 2014 Rome Web Awards. Season 2 was released in Spring 2014 on the show's official website whilewaitingforgodot.com.[^225]

## Place in Beckett's work

Although not his favourite among his plays, *Waiting for Godot* was the work which brought Beckett fame and financial stability and as such it always held a special place in his affections. "When the manuscript and [rare books dealer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_collecting "Book collecting"), Henry Wenning, asked him if he could sell the original French manuscript for him, Beckett replied: 'Rightly or wrongly have decided not to let *Godot* go yet. Neither sentimental nor financial, probably peak of market now and never such an offer. Can't explain.'" [^226]

## Related works

- [Racine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Racine "Jean Racine") 's *[Bérénice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9r%C3%A9nice "Bérénice")* is a play "in which nothing happens for five acts".[^227] In the preface to this play Racine writes: "All creativity consists in making something out of nothing." Beckett was an avid scholar of the 17th-century playwright and lectured on him during his time at [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin"). "Essential to the static quality of a Racine play is the pairing of characters to talk at length to each other." [^63]
- The title character of [Balzac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honor%C3%A9_de_Balzac "Honoré de Balzac") 's 1851 play *Mercadet* is waiting for financial salvation from his never-seen business partner, Godeau. Although Beckett was familiar with Balzac's [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose"), he insisted that he learned of the play after finishing *Waiting for Godot*.
- Many critics, including [Al Alvarez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Alvarez "Al Alvarez") and [Christopher Ricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Ricks "Christopher Ricks"), regard the protagonists in Beckett's novel *[Mercier and Camier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercier_and_Camier "Mercier and Camier")* as prototypes of Vladimir and Estragon.[^228] "If you want to find the origins of *Godot* ", Beckett told Colin Duckworth once, "look at *[Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murphy_\(novel\) "Murphy (novel)")*." [^229] Here we see the agonised protagonist yearning for self-knowledge, or at least complete freedom of thought at any cost, and the [dichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichotomy "Dichotomy") and interaction of mind and body. Mercier and Camier wander aimlessly about a boggy, rain-soaked island that, although not explicitly named, is Beckett's native Ireland. They speak convoluted dialogues similar to Vladimir and Estragon's, joke about the weather and chat in [pubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pub "Pub"), while the purpose of their odyssey is never made clear. The waiting in *Godot* is the wandering of the novel. "There are large chunks of dialogue which he later transferred directly into *Godot*." [^230]
- *Waiting for Godot* has been compared with [Tom Stoppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Stoppard "Tom Stoppard") 's 1966 play, *[Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosencrantz_and_Guildenstern_Are_Dead "Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead")*. Parallels include two central characters who appear to be aspects of a single character and whose lives are dependent on outside forces over which they have little control. There are also plot parallels, the act of waiting as a significant element of the play, during the waiting, the characters pass time by playing [Questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questions_\(game\) "Questions (game)"), impersonating other characters, at times repeatedly interrupting each other while at other times remaining silent for long periods.[^231]
- The 1991 West End production ([see above](#WestEnd1)), inspired Rik Mayall and Adrian Edmondson to develop *[Bottom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottom_\(TV_series\) "Bottom (TV series)")*, which Mayall described as a "cruder cousin" to *Godot*.[^232]

## Works inspired by Godot

- An unauthorised [sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequel "Sequel") was written by [Miodrag Bulatović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miodrag_Bulatovi%C4%87 "Miodrag Bulatović") in 1966: *Godo je došao* (*Godot Arrived*). It was translated from [Serbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_language "Serbian language") into German (*Godot ist gekommen*) and French. The playwright presents Godot as a baker who ends up being condemned to death by the four main characters. Since it turns out he is indestructible, Lucky declares him non-existent. Although Beckett was noted for disallowing productions that took even slight liberties with his plays, he let this pass without incident but not without comment. [Ruby Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_Cohn "Ruby Cohn") writes: "On the flyleaf of my edition of the Bulatović play, Beckett is quoted: 'I think that all that has nothing to do with me.'" [^233]
- [Alan Titley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Titley "Alan Titley") 's Irish-language sequel *Tagann Godot* (Godot Arrives) was written for [Oireachtas na Gaeilge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas_na_Gaeilge "Oireachtas na Gaeilge") in 1987 and produced as a [radio play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_play "Radio play") by [RTÉ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89 "RTÉ") and on stage in 1990 at the [Peacock Theatre, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_Theatre,_Dublin "Peacock Theatre, Dublin") directed by [Tómas Mac Anna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%B3mas_Mac_Anna "Tómas Mac Anna").[^234]
- In the late 1990s an unauthorised sequel was written by [Daniel Curzon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Curzon "Daniel Curzon") entitled *Godot Arrives*. Máirtín Coilféir finds similarities to Titley's work, of which Curzon was unaware.[^235]
- A radical transformation was written by Bernard Pautrat, performed at [Théâtre National de Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_of_Strasbourg "National Theatre of Strasbourg") in 1979–1980: *Ils allaient obscurs sous la nuit solitaire (d'après 'En attendant Godot' de Samuel Beckett)* (*They Went Dark Under the Lonely Night (based on 'Waiting for Godot' by Samuel Beckett*)). It features not four actors and the brief appearance of a fifth one (as in Beckett's play), but ten actors. Four of them bore the names of Gogo, Didi, Lucky and Pozzo. The dialogue, consisting of extensive quotations from the original, was distributed in segments among the ten actors, not necessarily following the order of the original." [^236]
- [Gujarati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarati_language "Gujarati language") playwright [Labhshankar Thakar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labhshankar_Thakar "Labhshankar Thakar"), along with Subhash Shah, wrote a play *Ek Undar ane Jadunath* (*A Rat and Jadunath*) based on *Godot* in 1966.[^237]
- In 2006, John Griffin wrote and produced a play titled *Godot Has Left the Building*, which serves as a modern-day homage to Beckett's *Waiting for Godot*. Set in a post-apocalyptic landscape filled with the remnants of modern technology, the play follows two men, Sebastian and Joe, as they struggle to find meaning in their desolate world. The play retains the absurdist themes of its predecessor, focusing on existential despair and the search for purpose in a seemingly indifferent universe. Unlike *Waiting for Godot*, which leaves many questions unanswered, *Godot Has Left the Building* is noted for its more direct confrontation with the futility of waiting and the inevitability of meaninglessness. The play received mixed reviews, with some critics praising its atmosphere and performances, while others criticized it for not offering the same depth as Beckett's original work.[^238] [^239] [^240]
- [Antoinette Nwandu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoinette_Nwandu "Antoinette Nwandu") 's play *Pass Over* takes inspiration from *Waiting for Godot*; it is set instead on a street corner in Chicago with two young black men, with appearances from a white man and an officer. It was made into a [film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pass_Over "Pass Over") directed by [Spike Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spike_Lee "Spike Lee") in 2018.[^241]
- In 2019, [Mister & Mischief](https://misterandmischief.fun/shows/escape-from-godot) created Escape from Godot, where an audience of eight participants must work together to explore the space, solve puzzles, call cues, and watch the performance in order to escape before "lawyers arrive to sue everyone in the theater for the entirely unauthorized and disrespectful production".[^242]
